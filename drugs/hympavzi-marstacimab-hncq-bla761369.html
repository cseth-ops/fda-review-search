Nonclinical pharmacology / toxicology

13.1. Summary Review of Studies Submitted With the Investigational New Drug Application

13.1.1. Pharmacology (Primary and Secondary) Tissue factor (TF) is a procoagulant protein located outside the vasculature that is released upon tissue injury to prevent excessive hemorrhage. Tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI) is a plasma serine protease inhibitor of TF. Marstacimab (PF-06741086) is a fully humanized immunoglobulin G1 (IgG1) monoclonal antibody that targets the K2 domain of TFPI and reduces TFPI activity (see Figure 12). By blocking TFPI, marstacimab enhances TF activity and promotes the extrinsic coagulation process in participants with hemophilia A and B (i.e., those with genetic deficiency in the intrinsic coagulation pathway, in response to damage to the endothelial wall of blood vessels). Given that TF and TFPI are released outside the vasculature in response to injury, they are considered part of the extrinsic coagulation pathway. Both the extrinsic and intrinsic pathways converge on a common pathway beginning with the activation of factor X.

Inhibition and neutralization of TFPI in the extrinsic pathway by marstacimab is thought to reduce the negative feedback on FVIIa/TF/Factor X (FXa) complex, promoting free FXa to enter the common coagulation pathway to promote clot formation. By enhancing free FXa in a manner independent of the intrinsic pathway, marstacimab can provide an alternative treatment option for patients with both HA (FVIII deficiency) and HB (FIX deficiency).

Marstacimab binds to TFPI at an epitope on the Kunitz-type domain K2 with similar affinity to the combined K1K2 domains (via K2), with a Kd of 2.63 and 3.7nM, respectively (Study #072904). Marstacimab has similar binding affinities to human, monkey, rabbit, rat, and mouse combined K1K2 domains (Table 41). Binding affinity of marstacimab to TFPI K2 of the combined K1K2 domains relative to concizumab). The triple Fc mutations caused marstacimab to have similar or lower binding to FcɣR than an isotype control IgG1 monoclonal antibody (mAb) lacking effector function, suggesting that marstacimab also has negligible effector function (Study #15GR003). TFPI is an endogenous serine protease inhibitor with at least three known Kunitz-type domains (K1, K2 and K3) separated by a short stretch of amino acids. TFPI is expressed by endothelial cells in α and β isoforms generated by alternative splicing of its pre-messenger RNA (Huntington and Baglin 2018). Mechanistic studies show marstacimab binds to the K2 domain of TFPI (Study #072904 & 121926). The enhanced coagulatory activity of marstacimab is based on binding to TFPI K2. By binding to TFPI K2, marstacimab prevents inactivation of both FXa and FVIIa, enhancing the extrinsic coagulation pathway, promoting hemostasis, and minimizing the need for FVIII or FIX. Safety pharmacology

Safety pharmacology assessments were incorporated into the 3-month toxicology study in monkeys (Table 49). Intravenous administration of marstacimab at doses ranging from 30 to 500 mg/kg/week and SC administration of 90 mg/kg/week to monkeys had no effect on respiratory, neurological, or cardiovascular parameters. There was no change in QTc interval following intravenous administration of 500 mg/kg/week marstacimab in monkeys.

Nonclinical ADME / toxicokinetics

The pharmacokinetics (PK) and toxicokinetics (TK) of marstacimab were characterized following once weekly subcutaneous and intravenous administrations in both Wistar Han rats and cynomolgus monkeys. Single and repeat dose PK (Study #13MA070, 8299672) and repeat dose TK studies (Studies #20064198, #20062114, #20089324) showed marstacimab exposure increased with increases in dose in both rats and monkeys. Importantly, marstacimab is cleared by target-mediated drug disposition. Low doses are rapidly cleared by target, while higher doses saturate target-mediated clearance, leading increasing half-life (t1/2). The t1/2 for marstacimab at 1 mg/kg intravenous (IV) was 12.3 hours in cynomolgus monkeys but was extended to 118 hours at 10 mg/kg IV (Table 50 and Table 51). The volume of distribution (Vd) in the monkey was estimated to be 34 to 65 mL/kg, which is similar the Vd of other monoclonal antibodies.

Administration of marstacimab in combination with other compounds had no impact on marstacimab levels in rats suggesting drug-drug interaction is highly unlikely. No specific metabolism, protein binding, or excretion studies were conducted in nonclinical species. Marstacimab is not expected to bind to albumin or α-1 acid glycoprotein. Marstacimab is predicted to be metabolized by a proteomic catabolism. Clearance of marstacimab follows a target-mediated drug disposition (TMDD), which predicts that higher affinity will result in higher clearance. Since marstacimab has lower affinity to TFPI than concizumab, larger doses should be necessary to produce a similar pharmacodynamic (PD) effect as concizumab. TMDD theory also predicts that lower doses will be cleared more rapidly while larger saturating doses will lead to a longer half-life. The low potency of marstacimab relative to concizumab (7-fold lower) thus requires a larger dose to achieve therapeutic efficacy, which results in a longer half- life that allows less frequent dosing of marstacimab in both nonclinical species (once weekly) and humans (once weekly mg) compared to concizumab.

General (repeat-dose) toxicology

Marstacimab produced a clear pharmacodynamic response in mouse hemophilia models. While marstacimab increased D-dimer, a marker of blood clot formation, in healthy rats and monkeys, administration of marstacimab did not decrease prothrombin time, APTT, or increase fibrin. No histopathological evidence of bleeding was observed at 200-times the area under the concentration-time curve (AUC) at the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD) in the 3- month rat study (doses up to 1000 mg/kg/week) or 3-month monkey study (doses up to 500 mg/kg/week). However, minimal acute emboli were detected in the 6-month rat study in the lung at 60 (1F, 1M), 180 (4F, 2M) and 1000 mg/kg/week (1F, 0M) doses and in tail injection sites at 180 (2F, 1M) and 1000 mg/kg/week (2F, 1M). Neither the lung nor the tail injection site thrombi/emboli findings in the 6-month rat study were considered adverse based on (a) absence of secondary lung tissue injury, (b) lack of clear dose-response, and (c) absence of change in prothrombin time, a marker of extrinsic and common pathway integrity. Similar acute minimal incidences of lung emboli were observed at the low dose of 30 mg/kg/week in one monkey (3- month study) and in one male rat (pharmacology study) at 50 mg/kg. In none of the cases was there any evidence of secondary lung injury, suggesting the lung emboli likely occurred at a very low level or at the study termination, possibly due to combined effect of marstacimab plus stress (exsanguination). Despite the unclear relationship between lung emboli in healthy animals and marstacimab, the anticipated risk of thrombi/emboli in hemophilia patients cannot be dismissed.

In summary, the presence of marstacimab-related acute minimal lung and tail thrombi/emboli identified sporadically across studies, suggests that marstacimab contributes to a minimally pro- coagulatory state in healthy animals that remains in homeostasis under most conditions. Thus, the top weekly intravenous dose of 1000 mg/kg/dose in rats and 500 mg/kg/dose in monkeys were regarded as the no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) doses, with AUC exposures approximately 200-times greater than clinical exposure at the maximum recommended human dose (Table 55).

Carcinogenicity

As a biological compound, the genotoxicity of marstacimab was evaluated based on a weight of evidence (WoE) approach, the key points of which are highlighted below:

• Biological nature of marstacimab: Biologicals possess no inherent carcinogenic risk based on chemical structure or metabolism. • Mechanism of action/therapeutic target: Marstacimab is an inhibitor of TFPI, a blood clotting regulator with no identified role in carcinogenic pathways. • Toxicology data: No preneoplastic lesions, evidence of hormonal perturbation, or signs of immune modulation were observed across studies in animals. Based on the WoE provided by the Applicant, the Division agreed that marstacimab is unlikely to pose a significant carcinogenicity safety concern for humans and therefore considered a 2-year study in rats unlikely to provide additional useful information.

Reproductive and developmental toxicology

Study #00655204 / Title: A Fertility and Early Embryonic Development Study of PF- 06741086 by Intravenous Injection in Male Wistar Rats

Key Study Findings

• Males Wistar rats dosed intravenously once weekly with marstacimab up to 1000 mg/kg (total of 11 doses) showed no adverse effects on fertility. • Marstacimab treatment in males mated with treatment-naïve females did not affect pregnancy, implantation, or embryonic survival. • The highest once weekly dose of 1000 mg/kg marstacimab was accepted as the NOAEL. Although drug exposures were not determined, plasma concentrations of marstacimab measured at 5 minutes postdose supported a determination of adequate drug exposure, because values were consistent with exiting TK data. Based on existing AUC data, exposure at the NOAEL in the study was likely 201-times the exposure at the MRHD of 300 mg per week on an AUC basis.
