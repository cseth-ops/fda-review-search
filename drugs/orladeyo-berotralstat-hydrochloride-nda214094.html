Nonclinical pharmacology / toxicology

Executive Summary The nonclinical program of berotralstat consists of studies of its pharmacology, pharmacokinetics (PK), and toxicology. Pharmacology studies showed that berotralstat inhibited kallikrein activity across species. PK studies showed that orally administered berotralstat is bioavailable in animals and humans. There are no major and unique berotralstat metabolites in humans. Toxicology studies evaluated the general toxicity, genetic toxicity, carcinogenicity, and reproductive and developmental toxicity of berotralstat. General toxicity studies included chronic oral toxicity studies in rats and monkeys (treatment durations of up to 26 and 39 weeks, respectively). Genetic toxicity was evaluated by means of a standard battery of assays. Carcinogenicity studies included studies of oral berotralstat with a traditional 2-year bioassay in rats and a 26-week study in transgenic mice. Reproductive and developmental

studies included teratology studies in rats and rabbits, a fertility and early embryonic developmental study in rats, and a peri- and post-natal development (PPND) study in rats.

General (repeat-dose) toxicology

Repeat-dose toxicity studies of berotralstat of durations of up to 6 months in rats and 9 months in monkeys have been completed to evaluate general toxicity. Studies of durations of up to 6 months and 13 weeks in rats and monkeys, respectively, were reviewed previously. See the nonclinical review by Dr. Luqi Pei on July 19, 2017, in IND 135058 (Document Archiving, Reporting, and Regulatory Tracking System [DARRTS] number 4126737). This review evaluates two 9-month oral toxicity studies of berotralstat in monkeys (Studies 8001292 and 806-162). These studies were completed by two different laboratories at different times and had no overlap in berotralstat doses. Specifically, Studies 8001292 and 806-162 were completed in 2015 and 2016, respectively. The berotralstat doses were 0, 1.0, 2.5, 7.5 and 20 mg/kg/day in Study 8001292 and 0, 30, 55, and 80 mg/kg/day in Study 806-162. The goal of the second study was to identify the target organs of toxicity of berotralstat because the first study showed no treatment-related effects.

Carcinogenicity

No evidence of tumorigenicity was found in mice or rats. The carcinogenicity potential of berotralstat was evaluated in a 2-year traditional bioassay in rats and a 26-week study in B6F1 Tg.RasH2 mice. Wistar rats (60/sex/dose) were dosed by oral gavage with 0, 3, 8 and 20 mg/kg berotralstat once daily for 104 weeks. Mice (25/sex/dose) were dosed by oral gavage with 0, 8, 20, and 50 mg/kg berotralstat once daily for 26 weeks. Neither study revealed treatment- related increases in tumor incidences.

Genetic Toxicity

Berotralstat produced negative results in the bacterial reverse gene mutation assay, an in vitro chromosomal aberration assay in human peripheral blood lymphocytes, and an in vivo micronucleus assay in rats.

Reproductive and Developmental Toxicity

Berotralstat was nonteratogenic in pregnant rats and rabbits dosed with the drug during the organogenesis period. Berotralstat did not affect the fertility or PPND of rats.

Others

Toxicity studies indicated that berotralstat can result in phospholipidosis and affect the cardiovascular (CV) system of animals. 5.1.4.1. Phospholipidosis Repeat-dose administration of berotralstat can cause phospholipidosis in rats and monkeys. Phospholipidosis-related findings included vacuoles in the epithelium and mucosa of the intestine, kidney, and choroid plexus (rats); foamy macrophages and histiocytes in the spleen, thymus, and intestine; and foamy Kupffer cells in the liver. Phospholipidosis was confirmed by electronic microscopic examination of Kupffer cells (myelinosomes) in the liver and the urinary level of di-22:6-bis(monoacylglycol) phosphate, a biomarker of phospholipidosis. Phospholipidosis is a known drug-storage mechanism. There is no evidence that berotralstat causes phospholipidosis in humans. 5.1.4.2. Cardiovascular Effects Berotralstat prolonged the QT interval in monkeys and humans. Berotralstat affected the conductance of potassium and sodium channels and the membrane action potential in vitro. Berotralstat also affected blood pressure, heart rate, and electrocardiogram (ECG) (i.e., slight increases in the RR, PR, QT, and QTc intervals, and in the QRS duration) in monkeys. In humans, berotralstat at an oral dose of 450 mg (maximum concentration [Cmax] 191 ng/mL) prolongs the QT interval by approximately 16 ms. A thorough QT study of the effect of berotralstat on the QT interval in humans has been completed.

Integrated nonclinical assessment

The nonclinical program of berotralstat consists of studies of pharmacology, pharmacokinetics, and toxicology. The toxicology program consists of studies of general toxicity (chronic oral toxicity studies in rats and monkeys), genetic toxicity (standard battery of testing assays), carcinogenicity (oral traditional 2-year bioassay in rats and a 26-week study in a transgenic mouse model), and reproductive and developmental toxicity (teratology studies in rats and rabbits, a fertility and early embryofetal developmental study in rats, and a peri- and post-natal development [PPND] study in rats). 16.2.1.2. Pharmacology Berotralstat decreases vascular permeability and edema in patients with hereditary angioedema (HAE) by reducing the blood level of bradykinin, a potent vasodilatory agent. Berotralstat decreases the bradykinin level by inhibiting kallikrein (inhibition constant 0.44nM), which cleaves and releases bradykinin from high-molecular-weight kininogen. The half-maximal inhibitory concentration for kallikrein activity in activated human, monkey, and rat plasma is 5.4, 5.2, and 7.2nM, respectively. Berotralstat inhibited currents of K+, Na+, and Ca++ channels in vitro and prolonged QT intervals in monkeys and humans. It also affected duration and amplitude of action potential in vitro. The effect of berotralstat on QT has been thoroughly investigated in clinical studies. See the section on effects on the Cardiovascular System. 16.2.1.3. Pharmacokinetics and Toxicokinetics Berotralstat was rapidly absorbed after oral administration to animals. The plasma drug level generally increased in proportion to the administered dose. There were no significant difference in plasma drug level between sexes. Oral bioavailability was 33% and 45% in rats and

monkeys, respectively. The half-life was 18.5, 22.7, and 93 hours in rats, monkeys, and humans, respectively. The tissues showing the highest maximum concentrations of radioactivity were the liver, kidney medulla, spleen, adrenal glands, kidneys, lungs, and pituitary gland. Benefit-risk

Benefit-Risk Summary and Assessment Berotralstat is an orally administered inhibitor of active plasma kallikrein for prophylaxis to prevent attacks of hereditary angioedema (HAE) in patients 12 years of age and older. The recommended regulatory action of this NDA is Approval. Hereditary angioedema (HAE) is a rare, autosomal dominant, inherited condition characterized by intermittent, unpredictable attacks of angioedema. HAE is caused by mutations in the SERPING1 gene, resulting in deficient or dysfunctional C1-esterase inhibitor (C1-INH) protein, a serine protease inhibitor. Absence of functional C1-INH leads to dysregulation of the contact system, unchecked kallikrein activity, and ultimately widespread release of bradykinin, which increases vascular permeability causing the characteristic swelling of acute HAE attacks. Although the exact prevalence is unknown, HAE is estimated to affect 1 in 10,000 to 50,000 individuals worldwide (approximately 6,000 to 10,000 individuals in the United States) and is categorized as an orphan disease. Acute attacks of HAE are potentially life-threatening, particularly in cases of laryngeal edema resulting in airway compromise. Attacks at other anatomic sites (e.g., gastrointestinal [GI] tract, genitourinary tract, and skin on the hands, feet, and face) can cause disabling pain and significant morbidity. These attacks are highly variable in frequency and location among individuals and even within a given individual. Patients with particularly severe and/or frequent attacks may be treated with prophylactic therapies to prevent or reduce acute attacks. While the context of available therapies for prophylaxis as well as treatment of acute attacks has expanded greatly in the last decade, most therapies require subcutaneous injection or intravenous infusion. Danazol, an attenuated androgen, is the only oral therapy approved for HAE prophylaxis; however, safety and tolerability issues limit its use. The efficacy and safety of berotralstat was evaluated in a single, well-controlled, adequately designed pivotal trial in Type I and II HAE patients 12 years of age and older. Patients had a minimum of 2 HAE attacks over a 2-month run-in period. Berotralstat treatment for 24 weeks versus placebo resulted in statistically significant and clinically meaningful reductions in the rate of HAE attacks. The 110 and 150 mg doses of berotralstat reduced the rate of HAE attacks by 30% (p=0.024) and 44% (p<0.001) relative to placebo, respectively. Efficacy was further supported by additional endpoints such as proportion of patients with at least a 50% reduction in HAE attacks compared to baseline. While the study demonstrated a significant treatment benefit in a patient population with frequent attacks who are likely to require prophylaxis, the treatment response was modest as few subjects achieved complete elimination of attacks. However, an oral therapy provides an additional, convenient option to the treatment armamentarium. The safety data submitted with the NDA were sufficient to assess the safety of berotralstat in the proposed HAE population.
