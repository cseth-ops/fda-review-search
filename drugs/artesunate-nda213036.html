Nonclinical pharmacology / toxicology

5.1 Executive Summary

Artesunate is a semi-synthetic derivative of artemisinin, (b) (4)

. The nonclinical safety profile consists of pharmacology, pharmacokinetics and toxicology studies in vitro and in vivo in rats, dogs, rabbits and monkeys. Pivotal studies with artesunate discussed in this review include repeated-dose IV studies (up to 28 days), embryo-fetal development studies conducted via oral administration, and genetic toxicology studies.

Although the subject of this NDA is IV artesunate, some of the submitted supporting literature and nonclinical studies were conducted using artemisinin and other artemisinin derivatives (i.e., arteether and artemether) to illustrate how artesunate compares across areas of known artemisinin class effects. Oral artemether, the only FDA approved artemisinin, is part of a fixed oral combination therapy with lumefantrine (Coartem®, approved 2009; Novartis Pharmaceuticals Corp) for treatment of uncomplicated malaria. Studies early in the drug development phase with artesunate were conducted primarily using a non-GMP formulation of IV artesunate produced in China by Guilin Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd. More recent nonclinical studies were conducted using a GMP Walter Reed Army institute of Research (WRAIR) formulation of artesunate The (b) (4)

pivotal repeated-dose toxicity studies in rat and dog were conducted using the to-be-marketed formulation of semi-synthetic artesunate in 0.3 M phosphate buffer. No nonclinical bioequivalence studies have been conducted between the semi-synthetic formulation and the WRAIR or Guilin formulations. In general, animal studies conducted using the (b) (4) (b) (4)

WRAIR and semi-synthetic IV artesunate formulations show a consistent pattern of toxicity across species (described below).

Pharmacokinetics

Artesunate (AS) is rapidly hydrolyzed to its metabolite dihydroartemisin (DHA) following intravenous administration, with a half-life of less than 0.5 hr for AS and DHA across all species. A mass balance study in rats indicate artesunate has a biphasic pattern of distribution, with an initial peak concentration of radioactivity present in plasma and whole blood 5 min after dosing, with a secondary drug plasma peak concentration occurring 3 to 4 hr after dosing, suggesting enterohepatic circulation or the presence of a second absorption phase. In rats, artesunate is present in whole blood at concentrations 2 to 4-fold that of plasma, with plasma protein binding capacities of 66 to 81% (comparable to human plasma protein binding of 63 to 75%). The rate and extent of artesunate metabolism to DHA varies by species, with extent of DHA formation in liver microsomes following artesunate incubation greatest in humans (85%), followed by rat (76%), cynomolgus monkey (59%), then rhesus monkey and beagle dog (both at 44%). Glucuronidation is a major metabolic pathway for both DHA, mainly via UGT1A9 and

UGT2B7. After IV administration, AS was mainly excreted via the urine (56%), with approximately 38% of the administered dose excreted via the feces. No nonclinical studies evaluating the excretion of artesunate in milk have been conducted.

Safety pharmacology

No studies have been conducted with AS on the inhibition of the tail current in human embryonic kidney 293 cells (transfected with hERG cDNA). The IC50 for DHA on hERG current was 7.7 ± 0.9 mcM. No adverse effects on cardiovascular function were observed in conscious beagle dogs following single or repeated (up to 27 days) IV dosing up to 40 mg/kg, approximately nine times the clinical dose based on body surface area comparison.

Neurobehavioral assessments in rats conducted after 26 days of repeated IV dosing of AS (3, 10, or 30 mg/kg) showed no biologically significant effects on parameters evaluated in a standard functional observational battery (e.g., gait and posture, motor movements, piloerection, etc.). Neurotoxicity is a class effect of artemisinins, with artemisinin and artemisinin-derived compounds (i.e., artemether and arteether) displaying a pattern of damage to specific brain- stem nuclei in experimental animals when administered in intoxication or lethal doses. The extent of neurotoxicity seen following administration of artemisinins appears to be dependent on the specific compound, dose, vehicle, and route of administration. Intramuscular administration of arteether causes widespread neuronal necrosis and gliosis in monkey and degenerative lesions in the trapezoid nucleus in rat. Brain histopathologic evaluations were unremarkable in monkeys and rats following repeated IV artesunate exposure (7 days) at doses that were 4.3 and 10-fold clinically relevant doses based on body surface area comparisons. Additional comparative studies reported in the literature report no behavioral or histopathological changes in brainstem nuclei of rats administered artesunate IM daily for seven days whereas rats receiving an equivalent molar concentration of arteether intramuscularly displayed significant behavioral deficits with histopathological evidence of chromatolysis, pyknosis and gliosis in brainstem nuclei.

General (repeat-dose) toxicology

The hematopoietic system was the primary target organ of toxicity in rats and dogs. A dose- dependent anemia characterized by reductions in hematocrit and red blood cells, increased mean corpuscular volume, reticulocyte count and percentage, and a moderate to marked leukocytosis was observed after 28 days of repeated IV dosing in rats at doses 0.7 to 2-fold the clinical dose based on body surface area comparisons. Hematologic changes were accompanied by erythroid hyperplasia in the spleen, bone marrow and kidneys. Reductions in body weight were also observed, with deaths occurring in 1 animal each from the mid and high dose groups. Histopathologic and hematologic changes were comparable to controls at the end of recovery, indicating reversibility of effects. The NOAEL was 3 mg/kg/day, a dose equivalent to 0.2 that of the clinical dose based on body surface area comparisons. Differences in IV artesunate formulation (i.e., versus semi-synthetic) are not likely to impact toxicity (b) (4)

given similar findings were observed in dogs administered the WRAIR formulation and the

semi-synthetic formulation. Repeated intravenous administration of 20 to 50 mg/kg IV artesunate to dogs for 14 days resulted in a reduction of red blood cells, hemoglobin, hematocrit and absolute reticulocyte count with no changes in histopathology. The NOAEL for this study was 10 mg/kg (approximately 2-fold the clinical dose based on body surface area comparison). Artesunate hematologic toxicities were dose dependent in the 28-day study, with anemia occurring at lower doses (5 mg/kg) compared with the 14-day study. In addition to reductions in red blood cells and hemoglobin, animals also had increased leukocytes and fibrinogen, indicating an inflammatory response. Histopathologic changes included extramedullary hematopoiesis in liver and spleen and erythropoietic hyperplasia in the bone marrow of the sternum and femur. Reversibility of hematologic and histopathologic changes was evident following a 28-day recovery period. No NOAEL was determined given hematologic and histopathologic changes were observed in all dose groups in the 28-day dog study. The lack of a NOAEL in the 28-day study is not of appreciable concern since this product is indicated for short duration of use (3 to 7 days) and hematologic changes observed were characteristic of artemisinin antimalarial drugs and findings were reversible.

Reproductive and developmental toxicology

No fertility studies with IV artesunate have been conducted. No significant changes in testicular histology, sperm motility, counts or morphology were observed in rats and dogs following 28 days of repeated dosing with IV artesunate. Histopathological changes of the seminiferous tubules and altered spermatogenesis (increased percentage of abnormal sperm and/or decreased sperm motility and viability) in rats and mice have been reported in the literature following repeated oral or intraperitoneal artesunate administration (3 days to 6 weeks) at less than clinically relevant doses. Given the conflicting findings in animals and lack of any formal fertility study(ies), the risk that IV artesunate administration poses to human fertility is uncertain.

Pivotal GLP embryofetal developmental toxicity studies have not been conducted with IV artesunate. A single intravenous administration of artesunate to pregnant rats on day 11 of pregnancy displayed a steep dose response curve for embryotoxicity, with a NOAEL of 0.75 mg/kg and the next highest dose tested of 1.5 mg/kg (0.1 times the recommended human dose based on body surface area comparison) inducing complete embryolethality in the absence of

maternal toxicity. A mass balance study conducted in pregnant rats demonstrated accumulation of artesunate in feto-placental tissues following a single administration of [ 14C]­ artesunate on day 11 of pregnancy at a dose 0.3 times the recommended human dose based on body surface area comparison. When given intramuscularly during organogenesis in rat, artesunate induced high levels of postimplantation loss, with complete resorption of litters seen at 0.75 mg/kg, 0.05-fold the clinical dose based on body surface area comparisons (fetal LD50 = 0.61 mg/kg/day; 95% CI: 0.59-0.63). Given the extent of embryolethality observed following a single administration, embryo-fetal development studies conducted with IV AS cannot feasibly be conducted at doses that would be clinically relevant. Embryo-fetal development studies have been conducted using oral artesunate, which has a bioavailability of less than 1% in the pregnant rat. Oral administration of artesunate during organogenesis caused a significant increase in post-implantation loss in pregnant rats, rabbits and cynomolgus monkeys at significantly less than clinically relevant doses based on body surface area comparisons. Surviving rat and rabbit fetuses displayed a spectrum of cardiovascular (ventricular septal defects, retro-esophageal right subclavian artery arising from the descending aorta) and skeletal malformations (e.g., ribs, long bone, and scapulae). Rabbit fetuses also displayed an increased incidence of brain abnormalities, including severely dilated brain ventricles and pons with absent areas. Embryolethality and/or fetal malformations following oral artesunate administration occurred in the absence of maternal toxicity. In rats and monkeys, the critical window for oral artesunate exposure associated embryolethality and developmental toxicity occurs early during organogenesis, at time periods roughly equivalent to the human first trimester.

Fertility studies have not been conducted with artesunate. Embryo-fetal development

Reviewer comment: The oral bioavailability of artesunate in the pregnant rat is < 1% and the extent to which artesunate and its metabolites distribute to gestational tissues following oral administration is unknown. Given the extent of embryolethality observed following a single IV administration in the pregnant rat, embryo-fetal development studies conducted with IV AS cannot feasibly be conducted at doses that would be clinically relevant. It is likely that the findings described below following oral exposure to artesunate during organogenesis

underestimate the risk of IV artesunate on embryofetal toxicity. Given that there are noted species-specific differences in the rate of conversion to DHA by the different artemisinin derivatives (i.e., arteether, artemether, and artesunate) and potential differences in the bioavailability of DHA and its distribution to gestational tissues following oral versus intravenous exposure, the findings of embryofetal development studies conducted using oral artemisinin derivates in evaluating the risk for IV artesunate use in human pregnancy is of uncertain value.

Prenatal and postnatal development

No pre- and post-natal development studies have been conducted with artesunate, nor are there any publications that evaluate the pre- and post-natal developmental toxicity of artemisinins.

Reviewer comment: The Applicant cited a WHO 2003 publication “Assessment of the safety of artemisinin compounds in pregnancy” which indicates that GLP studies on pre-and postnatal dosing and subsequent development of pups were conducted according to requirements of major regulatory agencies found no effect on postnatal development and maturation of offspring of dosed dams for any artemisinin derivatives, including artesunate. 8.1.1 SEAQUAMAT: A multicenter, open, randomized comparison between quinine and artesunate for the treatment of falciparum malaria in SE Asia. (Dondorp et al. 2005)

8.1.1.1 Study Design

This was a multicenter, open-label, randomized, active comparator trial conducted between June 2003 and May 2005. The trial was conducted at 10 sites in 4 countries in Southeast Asia: Myanmar (7 sites), Bangladesh (1 site), India (1 site), and Indonesia (1 site). It was sponsored by the Wellcome Trust, with a study coordinating center at the Wellcome Trust Unit/Mahidol University in Thailand.

The study objectives were to compare the mortality of severe falciparum malaria treated with parenteral quinine and artesunate (primary) as well as significant sequelae, recovery times, and development of complications of severe malaria.

Patients with suspected malaria were admitted and examined. Malaria diagnosis was confirmed using HRP-2 (histidine rich protein 2) immunochromatographic test (Paracheck, Orchid Biosystems, Goa India), designed to detect P. falciparum; confirmatory microscopy was performed after randomization.

Key eligibility criteria included the following: • Inclusion criteria o Clinical diagnosis of severe malaria o Positive test result for Paracheck o Age 2 years or older o Written informed consent by patient or relative • Exclusion criteria o Known allergy to artemisinin derivatives or quinine o Convincing history of artesunate, mefloquine or significant quinine (>2 g) intake in the previous 24 hours.

Eligible patients were randomized 1:1 to the parenteral artesunate or quinine group. The original randomization method was planned to use blocks of size 10 or 20, but unrestricted randomization was implemented according to the Statistical Analysis Plan (SAP).

Study drug (artesunate or quinine) was started with dosage based on body weight, as follows:

• Artesunate: Intravenous artesunate (Guilin No 2 Pharmaceutical Factory, Guangxi, China) 2.4 mg/kg on admission, 12 hours after admission, and thereafter once daily until able to take po.

Oral artesunate at 2 mg/kg/day was started once a patient was able to take tablets, to complete a total course (including parenteral) of 7 days; with total cumulative dose between 17 and 18 mg/kg over 7 days.

• Quinine: Intravenous quinine dihydrochloride (Government Pharmaceutical Organization, Thailand) 20 mg salt/kg loading dose infused over 4 hours (in 666 ml 5% dextrose water) over 2 hours three times a day until the start of oral therapy. Loading dose of quinine was not to be given if patient received partial pretreatment with quinine or mefloquine.

Oral quinine at 10 mg/kg every 8 hours was started once a patient was able to take tablets, to complete a total quinine course of 7 days.

During the study, supportive treatment was given to patient in line with guidelines provided (World Health Organization 2000). At the time of hospital discharge or death in the hospital, information on outcome was recorded. Complications and significant neurological sequelae were assessed in discharged patients.

Clinical safety

In this NDA, evidence for clinical safety of IV artesunate was based primarily on one randomized open label trial (SEAQUAMAT) and a retrospective analysis of data collected under an expanded access IND (CDC-060). Additional supportive evidence for safety was provided by two phase 1 studies in healthy volunteers (Studies 1128 and 1142), and two phase 2 studies in patients with uncomplicated malaria (Studies 1148 and 1263ab). Studies 1128, 1142, 1168 and 1263ab were sponsored and conducted by the U.S. Army and > 50% of CDC-060 was funded by the U.S. Army. The U.S. Army transferred rights for these studies to Amivas prior to the NDA submission. Study protocols, patient level datasets, annotated CRFs, and CSR were submitted for each of these studies. SEAQUAMAT was not conducted by the Applicant or the U.S. Army.

SEAQUAMAT was conducted in Southeast Asia and evaluated 730 patients aged 2 to 87 years who received IV artesunate, compared with 730 patients who received at least one dose of IV quinine (Dondorp et al. 2005). SEAQUAMAT included limited safety follow up as data were collected only during hospitalization, until death or discharge. Laboratory data were reported only for the admission timepoint. The primary endpoint was in-hospital mortality and secondary endpoints were neurological sequelae and recovery times (times to eat, speak sit and discharge), and development of severe complications. For adverse event analyses in SEAQUAMAT, the safety population in this section included all patients who received a least one dose of study medication. 15

Study CDC-060 presented data retrospectively collected from the first 102 patients who received IV artesunate under IND 76725 for the treatment of severe malaria in the United States. Datasets collected information on mortality, adverse events, and laboratory data until death or discharge, with limited additional information reported in patient narratives.

The phase 1 studies 1128 and 1142 in healthy volunteers followed patients until Day 15 and routine laboratory evaluations including hematology panels (Hb, Hct, platelets, reticulocytes, and neutrophils), bilirubin, AST, blood urea nitrogen, and creatinine, as well as ECG datasets (machine read). The phase 2 studies in patients with uncomplicated malaria included laboratory evaluations at Day 7 if previously abnormal (WRAIR Study 1168) and at Days 7, 14 and 28 (WRAIR 1263ab).

Benefit-risk

Benefit-Risk Summary and Assessment

In NDA 213036, the Applicant is seeking approval for Artesunate for Injection for intravenous (IV) use for the initial treatment of severe malaria in adults and pediatric patients. Artesunate for Injection is intended to be administered intravenously at 0 hours, 12 hours, 24 hours and thereafter once daily until oral therapy is tolerated and must be followed by a complete course of oral antimalarial therapy.

Severe malaria is a life-threatening infection and is a medical emergency. Most cases are due to Plasmodium falciparum. There are currently no approved drugs in the US for treatment of severe malaria. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the World Health Organization (WHO) currently recommend IV artesunate as the first-line treatment of severe malaria in adults, children and pregnant women. IV artesunate is currently available in the US from the CDC under an Investigational New Drug (IND) expanded access treatment protocol.

IV artesunate was superior to IV quinine, the standard of care when the study was conducted, for the treatment of severe malaria in a single, randomized, open-label, active-controlled trial in adults and pediatric patients in Southeast Asia (SEAQUAMAT), with supportive evidence from a published, randomized, open-label, active-controlled trial in pediatric patients in Africa (AQUAMAT). The primary efficacy endpoint for both trials was in-hospital mortality. In SEAQUAMAT, which enrolled patients two years of age and older with severe malaria, in-hospital mortality was 13% in artesunate recipients compared to 21% in quinine recipients [OR 0.59, 95% CI (0.44, 0.79), p=0.0002]. AQUAMAT, which enrolled pediatric patients less than 15 years of age, demonstrated a similar significant survival benefit for parenteral artesunate compared to parenteral quinine. The in-hospital mortality in the artesunate group (8.5%) was significantly lower than in the comparator group receiving quinine (10.9%), OR 0.75 (95% CI: 0.63, 0.90, p=0.0022).

Safety data for IV artesunate were obtained from SEAQUAMAT and an uncontrolled, open-label, retrospective study in U.S. patients with severe malaria treated with Artesunate for Injection under the IND expanded access treatment protocol (CDC-060). Additional supportive evidence for safety was provided by AQUAMAT, a study of artesunate dosing regimens in pediatric patients with severe malaria, EDCPT­ MMV07-01, a phase 2 trial of two dosing regimens of IV artesunate in pediatric patients with severe malaria, two phase 2 studies in patients with uncomplicated malaria, and two phase 1 studies in healthy adults. In SEAQUAMAT, the most common adverse reactions in patients receiving IV artesunate included acute renal failure requiring dialysis, hemoglobinuria, and jaundice. The most frequently observed adverse reactions in CDC-060 were anemia, elevation of transaminases, thrombocytopenia, hyperbilirubinemia, and acute renal failure. In SEAQUAMAT, the incidence of neurological impairment at the time of hospital discharge was higher in the artesunate group compared to the quinine; similar
