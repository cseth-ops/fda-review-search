Nonclinical pharmacology / toxicology

Executive Summary

The established pharmacologic class of nirogacestat is gamma secretase inhibitor. By inhibiting the activity of gamma secretase, nirogacestat blocks the proteolytic activation of Notch, which has a role in cellular proliferation, differentiation, survival, and oncogenesis.

Nirogacestat inhibited the activity of gamma secretase in vitro with an IC50 of 6.2 nM. In a cellular assay using human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293 cells that express a constitutively active NΔE mutant of Notch, nirogacestat inhibited the activity of Notch intracellular domain (NICD) with an IC50 of 7.7 nM. In human peripheral blood‐acute lymphoid leukemia (HPB‐ALL) cells that harbor Notch mutations, nirogacestat inhibited the activity of NICD with an IC50 of 41 nM and induced growth inhibition with an IC50 of 32 nM. Nirogacestat also demonstrated growth inhibition in various Notch expressing patient‐derived desmoid tumor cell lines in vitro with IC50s of 4‐160 µM. Nirogacestat exhibited anti‐tumor activity in numerous Notch‐driven tumor xenograft models in mice but did not induce tumor growth inhibition in a non‐Notch‐driven tumor xenograft model.

SpringWorks conducted stand‐alone GLP‐compliant safety pharmacology studies in rats and dogs to assess the effect of nirogacestat on the central nervous system, pulmonary function, and the cardiovascular system. There were no nirogacestat‐related adverse effects on the central nervous system, pulmonary function, or the cardiovascular system at single doses up to 500 mg/kg. Nirogacestat blocked the hERG channel with an IC50 of 1 μM; however, this IC50 is greater than 100 times the free Cmax in humans at the 150 mg BID dose. Consistent with these findings, nirogacestat did not have effects on QT prolongation clinically or in in vivo nonclinical studies.

The toxicity of nirogacestat was assessed in GLP‐compliant 3‐month repeated‐dose toxicity studies in rats and dogs. Shorter duration repeated‐dose toxicity studies were also conducted with nirogacestat and the findings were generally consistent with 3‐month repeated‐dose toxicity studies. Rats received up to 25 mg/kg (50 mg/kg/day) oral nirogacestat, twice per day, for 3 months, resulting in mean exposure levels (AUC) up to 3.4 times the AUC of 6740 ng.h/mL in patients with desmoid tumors at the 150 mg BID dose. Dogs received up to 5 mg/kg (10 mg/kg/day) oral nirogacestat, twice per day, for 3 months, resulting in mean exposure levels (AUC) up to 0.46 times the AUC of 6740 ng.h/mL in patients with desmoid tumors at the 150 mg BID dose. General (repeat-dose) toxicology

Nirogacestat was assessed in a series of nonclinical toxicology studies in alignment with applicable ICH guidance documents. The oral route of exposure was selected for these studies since the oral route is the intended clinical route of administration. The rat and dog were selected as the toxicology species based on nirogacestat PK and metabolism properties compared across species. The definitive studies were performed in the United States in accordance with the United States Department of Health and Human Services, FDA, United States Code of Federal Regulations, Title 21, Part 58: GLP for Nonclinical Laboratory Studies. Nirogacestat was administered to rats and dogs in repeat dose general toxicology studies up to 3 months in duration followed by a 1‐month recovery. A 1‐month mouse study was conducted to support a 6‐month carcinogenicity study in transgenic mice. Many of the Nirogacestat‐related toxicities were attributed to GS inhibition and disruption of the Notch signaling pathway. In the 1‐month mouse study, target organs in males and females included the small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, and ileum), liver, femoral physis, sternal cartilage, thymus, and sex organs in both male and female mice. The no‐observed‐adverse effect‐level (NOAEL) in the 1‐month mouse study was 20 mg/kg/day. In the 3‐month rat study, ovarian atrophy, alterations in the estrous cycle and decreased cellularity in gut‐associated lymphoid tissue (GALT) in females, and decreased cellularity of mesenteric lymph nodes in males and females at 5 mg/kg/day was observed. A NOAEL was not identified in this 3‐month oral toxicity study in rats due to these effects. In addition, all dose levels evaluated in the rat study showed chronic progressive nephropathy, pulmonary phospholipidosis, and salivary gland necrosis in a dose‐dependent manner. In the dog study, treatment‐related effects were present within the intestines, spleen, gall bladder, liver, kidney, testes, and ovary. The intestinal and liver findings were associated with generalized inflammation and associated clinical pathology changes in most of these animals. In the recovery dogs, only the intestinal, testicular, and ovarian findings were persistent but at lower severity, suggesting evidence of reversibility. Due to oocyte mineralization at the lowest dose in the dog study, a NOAEL was not identified.

The exposures at the NOAEL or lowest observed adverse effect level in the 1 and 3‐month studies were below the human steady state exposures after administration of nirogacestat at 150 mg BID. The carcinogenicity of nirogacestat was assessed in a 6‐month repeat dose study in transgenic rasH2 mice (20174162). Nirogacestat was administered orally BID (approximately 10 hours apart) for a minimum of 26 weeks to cByB6F1/Tg rasH2 hemizygous mice at dose levels of 5, 15, and 50 mg/kg/dose (10, 30, and 100 mg/kg/day).

Survival at 10 or 30 mg/kg/day nirogacestat ranged from 92% to 96% in males and from 96 to 100% in females and was comparable to controls, whereas survival at 100 mg/kg/day was reduced to 76% in males and females at study termination. Although there were no statistically significant effects on mortality when pairwise comparisons were made between the control group and 100 mg/kg/day males or females, there was a statistically significant dose‐related trend of increased mortality in the females. Administration of nirogacestat BID by oral gavage to Tg RasH2 mice for 26 weeks at 10 or 30 mg/kg/day resulted in no effects on survival or tumor formation. At 100 mg/kg/day, administration of nirogacestat resulted in an increase in the frequency of spontaneous hemangiosarcoma in males and females. There were no significant increases in new neoplasms associated with nirogacestat administration at doses up to 100 mg/kg/day. The systemic exposures in mice at this dose level are below those in humans administered nirogacestat at 150 mg BID. Based on the Agency’s feedback for Study 20174162 provided by email on 11 January 2022, SpringWorks submitted an updated waiver request for its proposal that a 2‐year rat carcinogenicity study was not required. As requested by the Agency, the updated justification addressed duration and exposure, carcinogenic potential, genotoxicity, indication and participant population, general toxicology study findings (e.g., histopathology, findings from the 26‐week mouse carcinogenicity study, and other relevant factors). Reproductive and developmental toxicology

The developmental and reproductive toxicology assessment of nirogacestat consisted of two separate fertility studies in male and female rats and a preliminary embryo‐fetal developmental (EFD) toxicity study in pregnant rats. The first fertility study was conducted by simultaneous treatment of male and female rats before mating (01214013). The second fertility study was conducted by treating male rats with nirogacestat then mating with untreated female rats, along with treated female rats mated with untreated known breeding male rats (01214026). Only Study 0124026 is tabulated below. Nirogacestat produced embryo toxicity when administered to pregnant rats, and impaired fertility in both male and female rats. In the EFD study in rats, nirogacestat induced significant embryo loss, early resorptions, and decreased fetal weights in surviving embryos. These negative effects on embryonic development were anticipated based on transgenic studies in mice demonstrating that the loss of Notch signaling is embryonically lethal (Donoviel et al. 1999; Swiatek et al. 1994). The changes in sex organs in nirogacestat‐treated male and female rats were also anticipated based on the known role of the Notch pathway in the ovary and testes. The ovarian changes in rats and dogs, along with altered estrous cyclicity, are likely due to inhibition of Notch signaling in ovaries, as this signaling pathway is critical in the regulation of mammalian folliculogenesis (Vanorny et al. 2017). Similar ovarian changes were observed with another GS inhibitor (Simutis et al. 2018). In the testes, the Notch signaling is critical for spermatogenesis (Murta et al. 2016). These effects on both male and female sex organs could explain the effects of nirogacestat on fertility indices measured in both male and female rats. Based on the severity of the embryo‐fetal toxicities, and the known requirement for the Notch pathway for normal embryo implantation and development, the weight of evidence provides justification for not conducting the definitive embryo‐fetal toxicity studies in rats and in rabbits, as well as not conducting the rat peri‐ and post‐natal development study. As shown by Donoviel et al (1999), mice lacking Notch activity by inactivation of presenilin 1 and 2 (upstream of Notch and required for GS activity), leads to perinatal lethality due to multiple early developmental effects. This finding supports earlier studies in mice where a loss of Notch1 is embryonically lethal (Swiatek et al, 1994). In addition, incubating zebrafish embryos with a GS inhibitor led to impaired somite segmentation (Geling et al 2002), which is similar to other lethal effects seen in the embryos of mice with altered Notch activity. rat embryo‐fetal development study, and the early embryonic loss in the fertility studies align with the known requirement of Notch for embryonic development. In addition, the exposures at which these effects occur in rat are below (≤ 0.1‐fold) the human systemic exposure based on AUC (12860 ng∙hr/mL) after administration of 150 mg of nirogacestat BID. Clinical efficacy

Studies A8641014, 14‐C‐0007, and NIR‐DT‐301 are the relevant studies used to support efficacy.

Study NIR‐DT‐301

Study Design The Applicant’s Description: Study NIR‐DT‐301 was a randomized, double‐blind, placebo controlled, Phase 3 study to compare the efficacy, safety, and tolerability of nirogacestat and placebo in adult participants with RECIST v1.1 progressing DT. This study consisted of 2 phases: a DB phase and an optional open‐label extension (OLE) phase. Nirogacestat was administered orally at a dose of 150 mg BID continuously, in 28‐day cycles. In the DB phase, participants were randomized (stratified by target tumor(s) location [intra‐abdominal or extra‐abdominal]) to study treatment (nirogacestat 150 mg BID or placebo) in a 1:1 ratio. Participants were stratified based on target lesion locations at Baseline (intra‐abdominal versus extra‐abdominal) as participants with intra‐abdominal tumors have been found to have a less favorable outcome as compared to extra‐abdominal tumors (Howard & Pollock 2016). If the participant had multiple target tumors located in both the

intra‐ and extra‐abdominal locations, the tumor was classified as intra‐abdominal. Participants remained in the DB phase until death, disease progression, discontinuation from study treatment for any reason, or the estimated number of progression events were observed and the primary PFS analysis was completed. The primary efficacy endpoint was PFS, which was defined as the time from randomization until the date of assessment of progression or death by any cause (whichever occurs first). Progression was determined radiographically by independent, blinded Central Imaging Review using RECIST v1.1 on target tumors identified by the investigator at Screening or clinically as assessed by the investigator and confirmed by blinded independent central review. Participants were eligible to enter the optional OLE phase if they were enrolled in the DB phase when the primary PFS analysis was completed, or they met the criteria of PD as defined by RECIST v1.1 (confirmed by central radiological review). During the OLE phase, all participants received nirogacestat 150 mg BID and remained on study until death, radiographic PD per RECIST v1.1, clinical progression, premature discontinuation, end of study, qualification for Continued Access Plan, or nirogacestat becomes commercially available. The DB phase of the study has been completed; however, the OLE phase is ongoing. FDA agrees with the description of the trial design for Study NIR‐DT‐301. FDA adds that patients who experienced clinical progression were not eligible to enroll in the OLE phase of the trial, and these patients were not unblinded. Patients who enrolled into the OLE phase were unblinded. Cycle 1 Day 1 (C1D1) visit for the OLE phase was within 24 hours of the end of treatment (EOT) visit for the DB phase of the trial, and assessments and determination of causality of all ongoing AEs and SAEs that occurred during the DB phase were conducted during the EOT visit prior to unblinding the patient.
