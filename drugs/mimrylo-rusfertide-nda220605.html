Safety pharmacology

Rusfertide was evaluated in a comprehensive safety pharmacology program including in vitro hERG assessment and in vivo cardiovascular, respiratory, and central nervous system studies. In vitro hERG assessment showed IC50 >30 µM (>56-fold margin), and in vivo studies in conscious telemetered monkeys showed no effects on ECG parameters or blood pressure at doses up to 10 mg/kg (8.4-fold exposure margin), confirmed in chronic toxicity studies up to 39 weeks. In rats, rusfertide produced no effects on respiratory function or neurobehavioral parameters at doses up to 25 mg/kg (20-fold exposure margins, each).

Nonclinical ADME / toxicokinetics

Following weekly subcutaneous administration, rusfertide exhibited dose-proportional pharmacokinetics with no systemic accumulation across all evaluated species and study durations. Male and female animals showed comparable pharmacokinetic profiles, with exposure differences staying well under 2-fold in both species. The 26-week repeat-dose toxicity study in rats (Study PTG-C-039) established a NOAEL of 10 mg/kg/week, yielding exposure margins of 4.8-fold to 11.1-fold over the MRHD exposure limits (Cmax of 1,302 ng/mL and AUC of 100,516 h·ng/mL). In the 104-week carcinogenicity study (Study PTG-C-105), the non-carcinogenic NOAEL of 3 mg/kg/week provided margins of approximately 2.5-fold (Cmax) and 1.0-fold (AUC) relative to the MRHD. In cynomolgus monkeys, the 39-week repeat-dose study (Study PTG-C-040) established a NOAEL of 2 mg/kg/week. This dose yielded minimal exposure margins of 0.77-fold to 0.96-fold over the MRHD, with the upper dose limits constrained by severe, pharmacologically mediated anemia (on-target dose-limiting toxicity). Because exposures to the major human metabolites M4 and M9 at the rat carcinogenicity NOAEL (3 mg/kg/week) fell below the threshold for automatic qualification (6.4% and 2.7% of MRHD exposures, respectively), dedicated qualification programs were executed: M4 was qualified in a dedicated 13-week toxicity study in rats (Study PTG-C-131), establishing a 3.8-fold margin of safety (MOS). M9 was qualified in the 13- and 39-week repeat-dose

cynomolgus monkey studies, where nonclinical exposures represented 90.1% and 83.5% of the MRHD M9 exposure, respectively.

General (repeat-dose) toxicology

Rats: • Rusfertide induced dose-dependent anemia in repeat-dose toxicology studies of 4 to 26 weeks duration. Anemia was characterized by decreased red blood cell mass (red blood cells (RBC), hemoglobin, hematocrit) with compensatory increases in reticulocytes, platelets, and red cell distribution width. All hematological changes resolved following a 5- week recovery period. These findings are fully consistent with the anticipated pharmacodynamic activity of rusfertide as a hepcidin mimetic. • In 26-week repeat-dose toxicology study, white blood cells (WBC) parameters demonstrated no dose-dependent or statistically significant changes except for decreased neutrophils, large unstained cell (LUC), basophils percentage, and increased monocytes in males at 10 mg/kg. The restriction of WBC changes to males despite higher rusfertide exposure in females suggest these changes are driven by the degree of anemia rather than direct drug effects and is consistent with a bone marrow shift toward erythroid hyperplasia and reactive monocytosis secondary to pharmacology-mediated anemia. • NOAEL was defined at 10 mg/kg/week across all repeat-dose toxicology studies yielding safety margins of 3.7x to 6.7x based on AUC comparisons. Dose-limiting toxicities observed at higher doses included severe anemia, decreased body weight gain, splenomegaly, and extramedullary hematopoiesis in the bone marrow, spleen, and liver. • Injection site reactions comprising fibrosis, inflammation, edema/hemorrhage occurred at all dose levels, including controls, and increased in severity with escalating dose and prolonged duration. The progressive but incomplete reversal of these findings suggests that the effects are at least partially rusfertide-related. • Unanticipated findings in males specifically prostate/seminal vesicle atrophy, mammary gland atrophy, and cecum inflammation were identified in the 4-week and 13-week studies but were not sustained in the 26-week study. These findings did not influence the NOAEL determination, did not adversely affect male fertility, and are not considered clinically relevant. • The 26-week study (NOAEL = 10 mg/kg/week; safety margin 3.73x) served as the basis for dose selection in the 2-year carcinogenicity study. A maximum tolerated dose of 3 mg/kg was selected as doses exceeding this level were anticipated to surpass tolerability threshold caused by progressive, rusfertide-related anemia. Monkeys: • Rusfertide induced anemia at all doses in repeat-dose toxicology studies of 4 to 39 weeks duration, as evidenced by decreased RBC with compensatory increases in platelets and reticulocytes. These findings were mostly or fully reversible following 5-week recovery periods. Anemic effect and associated findings were generally more severe in monkeys than in rats.

The carcinogenicity potential of rusfertide was evaluated in a 26-week rasH2 transgenic mouse study and a 104-week rat carcinogenicity study. The rat study included systemic exposure measurements of the parent compound and metabolites M4 and M9, which showed comparable parent:metabolite ratios to pivotal toxicology studies. Both studies showed no evidence of drug- related neoplasms.

Reproductive and developmental toxicology

Rusfertide was evaluated in fertility and early embryonic development (FEED) studies in male and female rats and embryo-fetal development (EFD) studies in rats and rabbits. FEED studies showed no effects on mating performance, sperm parameters, estrous cyclicity, or early embryonic development at doses up to 10 mg/kg in males and 3 mg/kg in females, despite non- adverse hematology changes consistent with pharmacology. In EFD studies, rusfertide was well tolerated in pregnant animals at maternal NOAELs of 3 mg/kg in rats and 1 mg/kg in rabbits but caused malformations in rats at ≥1 mg/kg (nasopharyngeal and CNS defects, eye malformations, fused ribs) and embryo lethality in rabbits at 1 mg/kg. The fetal NOAELs of 0.3 mg/kg in both species represent only 0.05x (rat) and 0.2x (rabbit) the human exposure at 120 mg, which are 20-fold and 5-fold lower than the maternal NOAELs, indicating rusfertide may cause embryo-fetal harm at therapeutic doses without maternal toxicity. A pre- and post-natal development study was not conducted and is not required for NDA submission.

The JAK2V617F mutation renders HSCs and their progeny hypersensitive to erythropoietin, thrombopoietin, and granulocyte colony-stimulating factor, resulting in constitutive, ligand-independent activation of downstream proliferative signaling and the simultaneous overproduction of red blood cells, platelets, and neutrophils. • Panmyelosis is a direct consequence of the clonal expansion of JAK2V617F-mutant HSCs and their differentiated progeny across all three lineages and explains why patients with PV present not only with erythrocytosis but also with thrombocytosis and leukocytosis. • PV is characterized by erythrocytosis, thrombocytosis, and leukocytosis. • The erythroid expansion characteristic of PV disrupts normal iron homeostasis through suppression of hepcidin. The markedly expanded erythroid compartment releases erythroferrone, which suppresses hepatic hepcidin production, increasing iron availability and further fueling red blood cell overproduction. This feed-forward loop between erythroid expansion, hepcidin suppression, and iron availability is a key pathophysiologic feature of erythrocytosis in PV.
