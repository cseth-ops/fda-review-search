General (repeat-dose) toxicology

Repeat-dose toxicology studies were conducted with oral and subcutaneous formulations of lenacapavir to evaluate toxicity from systemic and local toxicity and irritation. The most common toxicity noted among all of the toxicology studies was injection site reactions. The acute toxicity and tolerability of lenacapavir was evaluated in single dose intravenous studies in both Wister Hanover rats and Beagle dogs up to 30 mg/kg. Infusion site reactions (cellular inflammation, thrombus, congestion/hemorrhage, edema, and fibrosis) were noted at all doses. Additionally, clinical signs of toxicity in rats included: red urine, ataxia, hypoactivity, vocalization, squinting and/or pale eyes, audible/labored/irregular breathing, and sternal recumbence. Clinical signs of toxicity in dogs included: skin lesions, intermittent tremors, vocalization, and/or discharge of the left eye and front foot. Pivotal repeat-dose toxicology evaluation for lenacapavir included GLP studies of up to 6-month duration in rats and 9-month duration in dogs via oral or subcutaneous administrations. The skin at the injection site was the target organ of toxicity in both animal species. Additionally, in the 9- month dog study using the clinical formulation, the liver and kidney were also identified as targeted organs of toxicity. Based on systemic exposure in the repeat-dose studies in rats via subcutaneous administrations, there was no target organ identified at doses up to 100 mg/kg. However, based on local toxicity, the injection site was the target organ identified in the rat studies via subcutaneous administrations in all studies. These injection site reactions were observed after the recovery periods, albeit at a lesser degree of intensity. Toxicities at the injection sites included necrosis, granulomatous inflammation, mixed cell inflammation, and/or macrophage infiltration. Based on systemic exposure in the repeat-dose studies in dogs via subcutaneous administrations, there was no target organ identified at doses up to 100 mg/kg up to 2 months. However, repeat- dose studies up to 9 months in dogs identified the liver, gall bladder, kidneys, and stomach as targeted organs of toxicity. Liver findings included bile duct degeneration and cell/bile ductule hyperplasia. Gallbladder findings included edema and hyperplasia detected at doses up to 411 mg/kg. Kidney findings included tubule degeneration and dilatation, and findings in the stomach included degeneration/atrophy of mucosa at the highest dose of 411 mg/kg. Additionally, based on local toxicity, the injection site was the target organ identified in all repeat-dose dog studies via subcutaneous administrations up to 411 mg/kg. Toxicities at the injection sites included necrosis, granulomatous inflammation, mixed cell inflammation, and/or macrophage infiltration. Reproductive and developmental toxicology

No lenacapavir-related effects on male and female fertility parameters occurred with up to 100 mg/kg lenacapavir when administered via a single subcutaneous injection to rats at exposures seven- and five-fold (in males and females, respectively) the RHD. No lenacapavir- related effects on embryo-fetal development (EFD) occurred at doses up to 30 mg/kg lenacapavir when orally administered to rats once daily from gestation day (GD) 6 to 17 at exposures 16-fold the RHD. In the rabbit EFD study, lower body weight gains at doses up to 20 mg/kg correlated to a dose- dependent decrease in food intake following intravenous administration of lenacapavir. Body weights returned to those of controls levels after 9 days without treatment. No other maternal or fetal toxicity occurred with up to the highest intravenous administration of 20 mg/kg at exposures 39-fold the RHD. In the pre-and postnatal development (PPND) toxicity study, lenacapavir was administered to pregnant rats via a single subcutaneous injection of doses of 30 and 300 mg/kg on GD 6. There were no maternal toxicities or effects on PPND at doses up to 300 mg/kg at exposures five-fold the RHD. Lactation endpoints were not measured in the PPND study.

Additional Toxicology Studies Lenacapavir resulted in local toxicity at the injection sites at concentrations up to 500 mg/mL in rabbits. Lenacapavir was not phototoxic, cytotoxic, or considered an eye irritant. Using in vitro skin models, lenacapavir was not considered corrosive but was considered positive in two of three skin sensitization assays. However, in the in vivo assessment, lenacapavir was considered negative for sensitization potential. In the clinical formulation for marketing, the hemolytic potential of lenacapavir is low.

7.2. Potential Risks or Safety Concerns Based on Drug Class or Other Drug-Specific Factors Lenacapavir is the first in a new class of antiretroviral drugs, so no previously described clinical experience is available to inform any specific safety concern regarding capsid inhibitors. Immune reconstitution syndrome has been reported with combination antiretroviral therapy, particularly in the initial phase of treatment, in patients whose immune systems develop an inflammatory response to indolent or residual opportunistic infections. This is also often particularly relevant in a heavily treatment-experienced patient population. Loss of virologic response due to drug-drug interactions has been included as a warning in labeling for other ARVs. For other ARVs that are administered in an injectable form, such as Cabenuva (a long-acting formulation of two drugs, cabotegravir and rilpivirine), injection site reactions are the most common adverse reactions. These were described usually (79%) as localized pain or discomfort. The Cabenuva label notes that “Other manifestations included nodules (14%), induration (12%), swelling (8%), erythema (4%), pruritus (4%), bruising (3%), warmth (2%), and hematoma (2%). Abscess and cellulitis at the injection site were each reported in less than 1% of subjects” (Cabenuva 2021). Serious post-injection reactions were reported in less than 1% of subjects within minutes after the injection of rilpivirine and begin to resolve within a few minutes after the injection. These reactions were characterized by signs and symptoms including dyspnea, agitation, abdominal cramping, flushing, sweating, oral numbness, and changes in blood pressure. Additionally, given the long-acting properties of Cabenuva, the label notes that “residual concentrations of both cabotegravir and rilpivirine may remain in the systemic circulation of patients for prolonged periods”; therefore, a warning is included that nonadherence could lead to loss of virologic response and development of resistance.

7.3. Potential Safety Concerns Identified Through Postmarket Experience Lenacapavir has not been approved in any country; therefore, there has been no postmarketing experience with lenacapavir.
