Nonclinical pharmacology / toxicology

13.1. Summary Review of Studies Submitted With the Investigational New Drug Application

13.1.1. Concizumab Pharmacology Concizumab binds to an epitope in the K2 domain of tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI) that overlaps with the interface of Fxa binding. The results of in vitro and in vivo nonclinical pharmacology studies are consistent with the proposed mechanism of action of TFPI neutralization, whereby the Fxa produced by the activated coagulation factor VII (FVIIa)/tissue factor (TF) complex (normally downregulated by TFPI activity) results in increased thrombin generation leading to increased clot formation and theoretically hemostasis. The rabbit and cynomolgus monkey were identified as relevant nonclinical species. No other nonclinical species were identified that expressed Kunitz domain sequences that contained the amino acid residues in the epitope of concizumab on the human K2 domain. The cynomolgus monkey and rabbit K2 domains have a 96% and 93% sequence homology to the human K2 domain, respectively. The binding constants (KD) of concizumab to rabbit, cynomolgus monkey and human TFPI are 0.22nM, 0.06nM, and 0.04nM, respectively (determined by surface plasmon resonance, SPR). The TFPI association constants (ka) for concizumab were 1.3x106, 4.2x106, and 1.7x106 (M-1×s-1) and the TFPI disassociation constants (kd) for concizumab were 2.8x106, 2.3x106, and 6.3x106 (s- ) in rabbits, cynomolgus monkeys and humans, respectively. These data suggest that concizumab binds to TFPI with high affinity and to a comparable extent in humans, cynomolgus monkeys and rabbits. Functional cross-reactivity was evaluated in a modified prothrombin time (PT) clot assay and concizumab was found to be functionally active against TFPI in plasmas derived from rabbits, cynomolgus monkeys, healthy humans, and FVIII-deficient humans (half maximal effective concentration (EC50) values were 0.49nM, 0.41nM, 0.56nM, and 0.62nM, respectively).

Safety pharmacology

Safety pharmacology studies were conducted as part of the pivotal 13-week toxicity study with an 11-week recovery in cynomolgus monkeys (See Study 209206 for a summarized report of findings in Section 13.1.2.1). In brief, neurobehavioral observations which included extended clinical signs (CNS endpoints), respiratory evaluations (visual appraisal of respiratory rate/depth) and a cytokine release assessment (IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, TNF-α, IFN-γ) were all unremarkable. Cardiovascular assessments (blood pressure, heart rates, electrocardiogram (ECG) intervals, and the morphology of the ECG waveform) and urinalysis were conducted during the pivotal 13- week and 52-week repeat-dose toxicity studies in cynomolgus monkeys, and no effects of concizumab were observed up to the highest doses assessed (200 mg/kg/QW, intravenous (IV) and 50 mg/kg/day, SC). Safety pharmacology assessments conducted in vitro consisted of human Fc receptor binding, antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity (ADCC), complement fixation and binding to peripheral blood cells and are described in detail below.

Carcinogenicity

As concizumab is not functionally active in rodents, standard regulatory carcinogenicity studies in rats and/or mice would not be expected to provide a valuable assessment of carcinogenicity. Therefore, the Applicant conducted a carcinogenicity assessment of concizumab using a weight of evidence approach (Study 321420) that considered the mode of action, a review of the relevant literature (that included an evaluation of >670 publications) and an evaluation of the nonclinical and clinical data, consistent with the guidance provided in ICH S6 (R1). A summary of the Applicant’s assessment is provided below. • Tumor growth and metastasis is a complex and multi-factorial process and the TF/Factor VII (FVIIa) signaling pathway is known to contribute to this process. However, physiological levels of Tissue Factor Pathway Inhibitor (TFPI) do not appear to influence these reactions. Instead, at physiological levels, TFPI (and by extension concizumab, a TFPI antagonist) influence signaling via TF/FVIIa/FXa and this signaling pathway has not been associated with tumor development. • The C-terminal part of TFPI has been suggested to play a role in reducing tumor cell proliferation, angiogenesis, and metastasis. These processes are not expected to be affected by the specific binding of concizumab to the K2 domain of TFPI. • The inhibition of TFPI by concizumab leads to increased thrombin generation downstream in the coagulation process. Thrombin has been reported to mediate signaling reactions implicated in cancer, and these reactions may proceed if/when hemostasis is restored in hemophilia. Therefore, concizumab (and, in general, any procoagulant agent) has the potential to enhance the effects of thrombin, including the prothrombotic state, observed in many types of cancer. • Tissue Factor Pathway Inhibitor (TFPI) is present in some cancer cell lines at varying expression levels, and increased expression of TFPI would be expected to dampen the procoagulant effects of TF. Therefore, it is conceivable that concizumab (as well as any other procoagulant agent) could enhance the hypercoagulable state often observed with cancer. Treatment of hemophilia patients with concizumab therefore has the potential to increase the risk of thromboembolic events in subjects with undiagnosed malignancies. • In individuals with partial TFPI deficiency (e.g., due to specific TFPI polymorphisms, factor V (FV) deficiency or intake of pharmaceutical estrogen products, i.e., oral contraceptives), the effect of reduced TFPI on general cancer risk in humans is considered predominantly neutral. • Concizumab is a first-in-class mAb directed against TFPI. The available literature reports from three other TFPI antibodies in clinical development did not reveal any information related to human cancer risk. • Concizumab has been evaluated in nonclinical repeat-dose toxicity studies in cynomolgus monkeys with daily subcutaneous administration for up to 52 weeks. In these studies, no signs of proliferation, neovascularization, immunosuppression, or organ weight changes were observed.

Fertility and early embryonic development

Consistent with the guidance provided in ICH S6 (R1), specialized evaluations of fertility parameters were included in the 26-week chronic toxicity study conducted in sexually mature cynomolgus monkeys (Study 211087 in Section 13.1.2.1). There were no notable effects on the menstrual cycle duration, the length, width and volume of the testis, total sperm count, sperm motility or sperm morphology in cynomolgus monkeys administered concizumab at ≤9 mg/kg/day (SC) or 30 mg/kg/week (IV) for 26 weeks.

Histopathological evaluations of the reproductive organs conducted during the repeat-dose toxicity studies (≤52 weeks, GLP) in juvenile and sexually mature male and female cynomolgus monkeys did not indicate any adverse effects on the reproductive organs.

13.1.2.4.2. Embryo Fetal Development In mice, inactivation of the TFPI-gene has been shown to result in embryonic lethality (Huang et al. 1997; Ellery et al. 2015) and total TFPI deficiency has not been described in humans (Wood et al. 2014).The Applicant suggests that, as placental transfer of IgG is limited during organogenesis in humans, these findings may not be relevant when administering a monoclonal IgG antibody (i.e., concizumab) to humans (DeSesso et al. 2012). It should be noted that an association of recurrent pregnancy loss was observed in women who exhibited TFPI deficiency in combination with an Activated Protein C (APC) resistance phenotype, and in women with anti-TFPI antibodies in combination with antiphospholipid antibodies, suggesting that TFPI may be critical to the maintenance of normal pregnancy in humans (Martinuzzo et al. 2005; Gardiner et al. 2006). The reviewer agrees with the Applicant’s assessment that the implications of blocking TFPI functionality with a monoclonal antibody in pregnant women are not clear.

13.1.2.4.3. Pre- and Postnatal Development No pre- and postnatal development studies have been conducted with concizumab as the hemophilia patient population is almost exclusively male.

13.1.2.4.4. Juvenile Animal Studies Dedicated juvenile animal studies were not conducted with concizumab, consistent with the guidance provided in ICH M3 (R2) 2. The Applicant contends that the hemostatic system was the only target organ identified during toxicity assessments. However, the reviewer does not agree with this statement based on the extensive immune-related and vascular toxicity observed in cynomolgus monkeys. The human hemostatic system matures during the early weeks and months of life, and the concentrations of most hemostatic proteins, both in full-term and preterm infants, are comparable to adult values by 6 months of age (Albisetti 2003; Chalmers 2004). Consequently, the Applicant argues that the toxicity of concizumab has been adequately characterized through the general toxicology program in juvenile (2 to 3 years of age) and sexually mature (3.5 to 6 years of age) cynomolgus monkeys.
