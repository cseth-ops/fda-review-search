Safety pharmacology

Safety pharmacology analyses of trilaciclib were conducted for cardiovascular safety in vitro for hERG inhibition and in vivo in dogs and for central nervous system safety in vivo in rats.

Central Nervous System

Rats were treated with a single iv infusion (30 min) of 0, 15, 50, or 150 mg/kg trilaciclib and observed for neurobehavioral effects for 24 h postdose (n=10 males/dose). The GLP-compliant study included standard cageside and clinical observations and functional observational battery (FOB) that included a variety of observations for activity/arousal, neuromuscular function, sensorimotor function, autonomic responses,

and physiological condition. The high dose of 150 mg/kg was a lethal dose resulting in four deaths during dosing and the remaining animals in the dose group were euthanized without conducting the FOB. There were physiologic responses of decreased mean body temperature and slowed/labored respiration in two of ten rats in the 50 mg/kg group which were reversed by 24 h postdose. There were no treatment related effects in the lowest dose and a NOAEL of 15 mg/kg for neurobehavioral effects was established.

Cardiovascular System

An exploratory (non-GLP) in vitro hERG inhibition assay showed 100 μM trilaciclib caused a maximum 62% hERG inhibition and no IC50 was calculated. A definitive GLP- compliant hERG assay in HEK293 cells a showed concentration-dependent increase in inhibition of hERG-mediated potassium currents. An IC50 was calculated at a nominal concentration of 9 μg/mL, which represents an in vitro IC50 of approximately 20 μM trilaciclib. The calculated IC50 for hERG inhibition is on the order of 20,000-times higher than the estimated 1 nM trilaciclib inhibitory effect on CDK 4/6 for the intended pharmacologic effect. hERG inhibition is an in vitro biological marker for QT prolongation in vivo, which was assessed directly in separate studies in dogs and no QT prolongation was seen in dogs treated with trilaciclib at approximate clinical exposures.

A cardiovascular safety pharmacology study in dogs treated with a single intravenous infusion (30 min) of 0, 5, 15, or 45 mg/kg trilaciclib showed an effect on heart rate. Heart rate increased 30 bpm at 15 mg/kg and increased 45-60 bpm at 45 mg/kg. Elevated heart rate was transient and recovered to normal rates by 2.5 h to 3.5 h postdose in 15 mg/kg and 45 mg/kg groups, respectively. There was no drug-related effect on heart rate at the low dose of 5 mg/kg. Trilaciclib treatment had no effect on QT prolongation, blood pressure, or heart rhythm at any dose. The NOAEL for cardiovascular effects was 5 mg/kg in the study.

Cardiovascular effects were also assessed in a seven-day dog toxicity study. ECG monitoring after dosing on day 7. There were no cardiovascular effects, including on heart rate, at up to 15 mg/kg trilaciclib. At the 15 mg/kg NOAEL trilaciclib exposure was approximately equivalent to expected clinical exposures with Cmax = 1840 ng/mL (1.5X MRHD), AUC = 2530 ng*h/mL (1x MRHD).

Nonclinical ADME / toxicokinetics

Trilaciclib total systemic exposure (AUC) increased slightly greater than dose- proportionally, and maximum plasma exposure (Cmax) slightly less than dose- proportionally, after iv infusion. Exposures were similar in males and females and there was no evidence of drug accumulation after repeated dosing. Drug half-life ranged from approximately 3 h to 10 h, with longer t1/2 observed with increased dose and duration.

TK summary (7-day rat iv exposure)

Functional Observational Battery

After the initial dose and near the end of the recovery period (Day 18), a subset of rats from all toxicology group animals (n=5/group) were assessed in a functional observational battery (FOB) to investigate CNS safety pharmacology.

Dose-related, reversible reductions in body temperature were the only findings in the FOB. Immediately after the 30 min drug infusion body temperature was reduced in MD (-0.4 °F to -0.5 °F) and HD (-0.5 °F to -1.1 °F) males and females. Body temperature was normal and consistent with control groups in the FOB on day 11 of the drug-free recovery period.

General (repeat-dose) toxicology

(b) (4) The proposed clinical treatment regimen is iv course of trilaciclib prior to chemotherapy treatment cycles that typically cause myelosuppression. The seven- day iv toxicity studies were considered the pivotal toxicity studies which cover the clinical dosing route and duration. Additional oral toxicity studies in rats (up to 28-days) and dogs (up to 14-days) assessed longer term trilaciclib exposures and confirmed similar total drug exposure from repeated oral and iv dosing. Dose-limiting toxicity in the 14-day and 28-day studies was consistent with exaggerated pharmacology and generally similar to the 7-day iv toxicity study results. Consistency across the 7-day iv (b) (4) and 2- to 4-week oral toxicity studies provided further support for repeated trilaciclib clinical dosing during multiple 4-week chemotherapy courses (which provide approximately 3-week trilaciclib dosing holiday per course of treatment).

Genetic toxicology

Potential for genetic toxicity of trilaciclib was assessed in vitro in Ames mutagenesis assays, in vitro micronucleus assay in human peripheral blood lymphocytes, and an in vitro H2AX phosphorylation assay in human fibroblast cultures. Genetic toxicity was not assessed in any in vivo assay (e.g., rat liver micronucleus formation in vivo to assess clastogenic potential). Trilaciclib was negative for mutagenic potential in the Ames assays and negative for clastogenic potential in primary human fibroblasts. Trilaciclib increased micronucleus formation in human peripheral blood lymphocytes in vitro, suggesting clastogenic activity. However, lymphocytes were shown to be very sensitive to the intended pharmacologic effect of G1 cell cycle by trilaciclib, so the increase in micronuclei seen in HPBL cells was confounded by direct drug-related effects on lymphocyte proliferation. There was no weight-of-evidence discussion about trilaciclib genotoxicity potential overall, however, no new hazard was identified since cell cycle arrest and post-treatment proliferation and repopulation of lymphocytes has been observed. The proposed indicated use in SCLC patients being treated with genotoxic chemotherapeutic agents also obviates a need for any further genotoxicity of trilaciclib or trilaciclib metabolites (e.g., M8) for the proposed use.

Carcinogenicity

Carcinogenic potential of trilaciclib has not been assessed in animals. The intended pharmacologic effect of G1 cell cycle arrest could theoretically protect transformed cells from programmed cell death and allow tumor progression. However, the intended effect of myelopreservation from blood stem cell cycle arrest prior to exposure to chemotherapeutic agents intended to kill rapidly dividing cells is indicated due to the preferential targeting of HPSC and lymphocyte cell populations. Importantly, trilaciclib (b) (4) clinical dosing is in patients who are receiving chemotherapy to target existing small cell lung cancer so there was little to be gained from assessment of carcinogenic potential in animals subjected to chronic treatment.

Reproductive and developmental toxicology

No fertility, developmental, or reproductive toxicity studies were conducted in animals. Prescribing information for U.S. listed CDK 4/6 inhibitors describes a risk of fetal harm to exposure during pregnancy due to the mechanism of action of the drugs. The intended pharmacologic effect of G1 cell cycle arrest in human peripheral stem cells and lymphocytes is indicated to preserve blood cell populations from cell death induced by chemotherapeutic agents. The same pharmacologic effect of cell cycle arrest in rapidly dividing cells in a developing embryo or fetus may be teratogenic. Indeed, various fetal malformations were observed in embryofetal development studies for listed CDK 4/6 inhibitors and teratogenic effects are described in the labels. Toxicity studies in juvenile animals have not been conducted. The proposed indication, small cell lung cancer, is not known to occur in children, so juvenile animal studies are not required to support pediatric use. The Division recommended the sponsor request a waiver for pediatric clinical trials, to which the sponsor complied and the Agency’s Pediatric Review Committee (PeRC) agreed, to a full waiver obviating the requirement to conduct pediatric trials.
