General (repeat-dose) toxicology

Repeat oral dose toxicology studies up to 26 weeks in rats and 39 weeks in dogs were conducted to support the chronic oral administration of aficamten intended for clinical use. Both species are considered pharmacologically relevant given the high conservation of the cardiac myosin motor between species and based on in vitro and single-dose in vivo pharmacology studies. FDA Assessment The heart was identified as the primary organ of toxicity in both species with repeated aficamten administrations. Across the nonclinical studies included in Table 51, both M1a and M1b metabolites generally demonstrated exposures exceeding 0.5-fold relative to the clinical exposure at the 20mg dose, a threshold typically warranted to achieve adequate nonclinical assessment for disproportionate metabolites. The exception was M1a exposure in male animals in carcinogenicity studies, where exposures were limited to 0.2-fold in the 6-month transgenic mouse study and 0.3-fold in the 2- year rat study. This lower exposure was attributed to two factors: cardiac toxicities associated with aficamten that limited the high dose selection, and the inherently lower M1a levels present in rodent species relative to humans, after aficamten administration. Given the higher maximum tolerated dose of aficamten in females, M1a exposure in females reached 0.5-fold relative to clinical exposure without tumor findings in the 2-year rat carcinogenicity study. Mechanistically the M1a metabolite is formed through first-pass metabolism by P450-mediated hydroxylation and is structurally very similar to aficamten, which was negative for tumor signals in 6-month and 2-year rodent carcinogenicity studies. M1a is pharmacologically inactive, and nonclinical rodent studies achieving exposures above 0.5-fold (including the 13-week rat study) showed no proliferative or preneoplastic findings of concern. The 2-year rat study, which reached 0.5-fold exposure, similarly demonstrated no concerning signals. Furthermore, M1a was determined to be non-genotoxic based on a comprehensive battery of assays and weight-of-evidence evaluation. Collectively, these data indicate minimal carcinogenic risk for this metabolite, therefore, we conclude that a dedicated carcinogenicity study is not warranted.

13.1.2.3. Drug-Drug Interactions Aficamten was not a substrate for OATP1B1/B3, MDR1/BCRP, or P-gp in vitro. In a transporter panel inhibition screen, aficamten and its major metabolites had no significant effects on the activity of OAT1, OCT2, OCT3, or OATP1B3. The IC50 for aficamten against OAT3, OATP1B1, MATE1, and MATE2-K were 12, 71, 36, and 83µM, respectively. In vitro CYP phenotyping, induction, and inhibition studies using recombinant CYP proteins, human liver microsomes, human hepatocytes, or a human microliver model were performed. Results from the studies collectively suggest that CYPs 2C8, 2C9, 2C19, 2D6, and 3A4 contribute to metabolic clearance of aficamten in humans. Aficamten did not significantly inhibit any CYP enzyme in in vitro assays.
