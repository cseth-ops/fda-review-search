Nonclinical pharmacology / toxicology

Executive Summary

Tebentafusp (IMCgp100) is a bispecific fusion protein that consists of a soluble T cell receptor (TCR) fused to an anti‐cluster of differentiation 3 (anti‐CD3) single‐chain variable fragment (scFv). The TCR domain binds a gp100 peptide (peptide fragment derived from the melanoma associated antigen glycoprotein 100, position 280‐288, YLEPGPVTA):human leukocyte antigen (HLA)‐A*02:01 complex. Glycoprotein 100 (gp100) is a type 1 transmembrane glycoprotein that is enriched in melanosomes. The established pharmacologic class (EPC) is bispecific gp100 peptide‐HLA‐directed CD3 T‐cell engager.

In an in vitro assay, tebentafusp bound the gp100 peptide:HLA‐A*02:01 complex with a mean KD value of 24 pM. Tebentafusp bound CD3 with a mean KD value of 38 nM. Average bifunctional binding of tebentafusp to gp100 peptide‐HLA‐A*02:01 and soluble CD3 was 88%. Tebentafusp exhibited binding specificity towards the HLA‐A*02:01 subtype, with EC50 values ranging from ~30 to 60 pM for HLA‐A*02:01‐positive cell lines. Comparatively, tebentafusp bound other HLA‐A subtypes with EC50 values of ≥166 pM. In HLA‐A*02:01‐positive cutaneous and uveal melanoma cell lines co‐incubated with peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), tebentafusp treatment led to a dose‐dependent increase in IFNγ release with EC50 values ranging from 0.89 to 121 pM. T cell activation by tebentafusp increased cytokines IFNγ, TNF‐α, IL‐6, IL‐2, and MIP‐1β. In vivo, the anti‐tumor activity of tebentafusp was evaluated in mice engrafted with HLA‐A*02:01/gp100‐positive Mel526 human melanoma cancer cells mixed with PBMCs isolated from human whole blood. In this study, administration of 5 doses of tebentafusp resulted in tumor growth inhibition compared to controls.

There are no pharmacologically relevant animal species to evaluate tebentafusp toxicity. In vitro, the scFv portion of tebentafusp bound to T cells in human PBMCs but did not bind PBMCs from mouse or monkey. Tebentafusp was evaluated in a series of in vitro studies to characterize potential cross‐reactivity in 35 normal human tissues by immunohistochemistry (IHC) and the potential for organ‐specific toxicities in functional assays using 13 normal human tissues. Tebentafusp did not show off‐target/cross‐reactivity in tissues outside of melanocytes, and alloreactivity and platelet activation were not observed.

Genotoxicity and carcinogenicity studies were not warranted.

Tebentafusp was not evaluated in embryofetal toxicity studies. In the reproductive toxicity assessment provided by the Applicant, the following points are considered: (1) gp100 is expressed embryonically in melanocytes found in the skin, inner ear, and eyes by 7 weeks of gestation; (2) MHC Class I classical proteins (including HLA‐A*02:01) and transporter‐ associated‐with‐antigen‐processing (TAP) proteins required for presentation of antigen on the cell surface are expressed early in embryonic development; and (3) CD3‐positive T cells begin to populate the periphery and are capable of being activated by around 12 weeks of gestation.

FDA agrees.

ADME/PK

Tebentafusp can only recognize human HLA; therefore, all nonclinical toxicology studies were conducted in vitro. Therefore, conventional in vivo animal models used to evaluate drug distribution, bioavailability, mechanism(s) of elimination, and potential risks of specific tissue toxicity are considered limited. Nevertheless, the nonclinical pharmacokinetics (PK) of tebentafusp were evaluated in four animal studies: non tumor-bearing and tumor-bearing severe combined immune deficiency (SCID) mice, C57BL/6 mice, and CD57 or HDD mice. These studies were non-GLP, PK and /or biodistribution studies and administered as single intravenous (IV) or subcutaneous (SC) dose. Preclinical biodistribution studies demonstrate that tebentafusp is being retained in the melanoma tumor for a significantly longer timeframe compared to other non-target tissues, with a half-life of tumour retention of approximately 24 hours. Observed pharmacokinetics for tebentafusp after IV administration were generally linear across dose levels tested in different strains of mice. Maximum concentrations (Cmax) and exposure (AUC) increased in an approximately dose proportional manner for dose levels from 0.005 to 0.05 mg/kg. The maximum concentrations were recorded at 15 minutes, as well as a secondary concentration peak at 1 hour, while the radioactivity was detectable in all samples up to 72 hours. The terminal half-lives calculated from the plasma radioactivity concentrations were also similar at each dose level. Tebentafusp exposure was maintained at measurable levels for 72 hours. Following SC administration of [3H]-tebentafusp to CD57 mice at 0.05 mg/kg, the maximum mean plasma radioactivity concentration was lower and measured later than that measured after the IV dose route at the same dose level, as expected due to the dose route. Concentrations then decreased and were still detectable in all samples at 72 hours. The terminal half-live calculated from the plasma radioactivity concentrations was similar to that calculated following the intravenous administration.

General (repeat-dose) toxicology

Tebentafusp can only recognize human HLA; therefore, all nonclinical toxicology studies were conducted in vitro. Human tissue and cell-based in vitro approaches were taken to understand potential organ-specific toxicities that may arise in the clinical setting with tebentafusp. These approaches considered the on-target/off-tumor potential effects, as well as the off-target/off-tumor effects (cross-reactivity) of tebentafusp. These studies are detailed in Secondary Pharmacology Section above.

Tebentafusp was also investigated in toxicology studies to assess its cross‐reactivity with normal tissues in a GLP‐compliant tissue cross‐reactivity study. The ability of tebentafusp to bind to a panel of 35 normal human tissues from 3 healthy donors was assessed using immunohistochemistry (IHC). Staining with tebentafusp was observed in the membrane and cytoplasm of lymphocytes throughout the human tissue panel examined. Similar staining was observed with the individual anti‐CD3 component of tebentafusp, while no staining was observed with the individual TCR component, indicating that this staining was attributable to the binding of tebentafusp to CD3. As CD3 is a T lymphocyte marker, this staining was expected. No other staining was observed across the panel of tissues tested, indicating no unexpected cross‐reactivity. It should be noted that the TCR targeting domain of tebentafusp recognizes very low target pHLA complexes (>5 pHLA complexes per cell), which are expected to be outside the normal range of sensitivity of the IHC method and would therefore inform on cross‐ reactivity above the threshold of detection of IHC only. Overall, the results of this study are consistent with published reports of gp100 being specific to melanocytes and melanoma cells, and not in other.

In general, and as noted by the Applicant, the use of IHC has limitations based on the low number of target complexes (i.e., IHC is not as sensitive a measure for this cross‐reactivity assessment). Tebentafusp staining was observed in the membrane and cytoplasm of lymphocytes in the mammary gland, gastrointestinal tract (colon, esophagus, small intestine, and stomach), kidney, liver, lung, lymph node, fallopian tube, prostate, salivary gland, skin, spleen, thymus, thyroid, tonsil, ureter, urinary bladder, and cervix (Study IM 1861). FDA agrees that this appears to be attributable to CD3 binding. Of note, brain was negative for staining, indicating tebentafusp activity at <1nM in 1/5 human astrocyte samples is likely of low clinical risk (Study IMC 1001).

Reproductive and developmental toxicology

Tebentafusp is a bispecific fusion protein for the treatment of unresectable or metastatic uveal melanoma. The targeting end is an affinity-enhanced T cell receptor (TCR) that binds a human peptide fragment of the melanoma associated antigen glycoprotein 100 (gp100) presented by human leukocyte antigen-A*02:01 (HLA-A*02:01), which is expressed on the surface of melanoma cells. The effector end is a single chain variable fragment (scFv) that binds specifically to human CD3 on T cells. Once administered, tebentafusp is expected to bind to melanoma tissue and stimulate the immune system to attack the target tissue. Due to the specificity for human targets, embryo-fetal development (EFD) studies cannot be conducted in nonclinical species. Therefore, a weight of evidence assessment was conducted to evaluate the risk of tebentafusp administration on pregnancy and embryo-fetal development. A literature search revealed that gp100 is expressed embryonically in melanocytes found in the skin, inner ear, and eyes by 7 weeks of gestation, and MHC Class I classical proteins (including HLA- A*02:01) and transporter-associated-with-antigen-processing (TAP) proteins required for presentation of antigen on the cell surface are expressed very early in embryonic development. In addition, CD3-positive T cells begin to populate the periphery and are capable of being activated by around 12 weeks of gestation. However, tebentafusp’s ability to directly affect embryo-fetal development requires that tebentafusp crosses the placenta and gains access to the fetus. The molecular weight of tebentafusp is 77 kDa, therefore, it is much too large to cross the placenta by diffusion. It also does not contain an Fc domain and, therefore, cannot bind to the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn), which facilitates transfer of IgG molecules across the placenta, nor does it have domains that could bind to other placental transporters. Thus, tebentafusp would not be able to gain access to or elicit pharmacologic activity directly in the embryo/fetus. There is also no evidence that

tebentafusp could interfere with implantation or the maintenance of pregnancy through its mechanism of action. Based on the results of this weight of evidence assessment, tebentafusp would not be expected to adversely affect embryo-fetal development or the maintenance of pregnancy when administered during pregnancy.

Biologics of this molecular weight may cross the placenta. Based on its mechanism of action, tebentafusp may cause fetal harm, particularly the skin, inner ear, and eye, if the developing fetus is exposed to tebentafusp.

Tebentafusp is a bispecific gp100 peptide‐HLA‐A*02:01 directed CD3 T cell engager. The applicant is seeking approval of tebentafusp for the treatment of HLA‐A*02:01‐positive adult patients with unresectable or metastatic uveal melanoma. The proposed dosing regimen is 20 mcg on Day 1, 30 mcg on Day 8, 68 mcg on Day 15, and 68 mcg once every week thereafter, via intravenous infusion over 15‐20 minutes.

Pivotal Study 202

Trial Design

The Applicant’s Description:

Overall design This is an ongoing open-label, randomized, multi-center study to evaluate the efficacy and safety of tebentafusp versus investigator’s choice (dacarbazine, ipilimumab, or pembrolizumab) in adult HLA-A*02:01-positive patients with mUM who had not received prior systemic therapy in the metastatic setting. Enrolled patients were randomized 2:1 to receive either tebentafusp (Arm1) or investigator’s choice (Arm 2), the latter of which was selected prior to randomization. Randomization was stratified by LDH status. The study schema is depicted in Figure 25. Arm 1 (tebentafusp): All patients randomized to Arm 1 received tebentafusp by intravenous (IV) infusion following the intra-patient escalation regimen. Patients received 20 mcg on Day 1, 30 mcg on Day 8, 68 mcg on Day 15, and 68 mcg once every week thereafter. Due to the anticipated cytokine release-associated toxicity with tebentafusp following the first 3 doses, patients were monitored for at least 16 hours after dosing as an inpatient following the weekly doses on Days 1, 8, and 15. Arm 2 (investigator’s choice): All patients randomized to Arm 2 received investigator’s choice of one of the following 3 options: dacarbazine at the standard dosing regimen in UM of 1000 mg/m2 given on Day 1 of each 21-day cycle; ipilimumab at the dosing regimen for unresectable or metastatic melanoma of 3 mg/kg given on Day 1 of each 21-day cycle for a maximum of 4 doses; or pembrolizumab at the dosing regimen of 2 mg/kg up to a maximum of 200 mg or 200 mg administered IV where approved locally given on Day 1 of each 21-day cycle. The preferred investigator’s choice agent was selected prior to randomization. No extended monitoring after dosing was required in Arm 2.

Treatment continued until radiographic progression (except for patients receiving ipilimumab), unacceptable toxicity, investigator decision, or patient withdrawal of consent. Patients receiving tebentafusp, pembrolizumab, or ipilimumab experiencing disease progression per RECIST could continue with treatment beyond initial RECIST progression if they met the prespecified criteria.

Choice of control group Prior to this study, no therapy has demonstrated a survival benefit in UM (Ramaiya, 2007; Augsberger, 2009; Khattak, 2013). In the absence of standard of care, options for UM patients have included PD-1 inhibitors, ipilimumab, or chemotherapy. Therefore, the comparator arm for this randomized study was an investigator’s choice with 3 options that included pembrolizumab, ipilimumab, and dacarbazine.

Eligibility criteria Key inclusion criteria included:  ≥ 18 years of age at the time of informed consent. • Histologically or cytologically confirmed mUM. • No prior systemic therapy in the metastatic or advanced setting. • No regional liver-directed therapy. • HLA-A*02:01 positive by central assay. • ECOG performance status score of 0 or 1.

Clinical safety

The clinical development program of tebentafusp in melanoma is comprised of 5 Immunocore sponsored clinical studies, including 2 completed studies (Phase 1, IMCgp100-01 [Study 01];

Phase 2 rollover study IMCgp100401 [Study 401]) and 3 ongoing studies (Phase 1/2, IMCgp100-102 [Study 102]; Phase 1b/2, IMCgp100-201 [Study 201]; and Phase 3, IMCgp100- 202 [Study 202]). Across these clinical studies, 587 patients with melanoma, including 410 patients with uveal melanoma (UM), have been exposed to tebentafusp monotherapy.
